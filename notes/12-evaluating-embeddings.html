12. Evaluating Embeddings & Their Limits

Intrinsic vs extrinsic evaluation, FastText, bias, and why static vectors aren't enough.

Part B · Neural NLP🎙 Neural NLP Lecture 4 (19 Oct, part 1)

In 30 seconds

  • Intrinsic evaluation tests embeddings on small sub-tasks (similarity, analogies, clustering, verb–object fit). Fast, but not proof of real usefulness.
  • Extrinsic evaluation tests them inside a real downstream task (sentiment, translation, NER), with embeddings either fixed or fine-tuned. Slower, but it's what matters.
  • No single embedding wins everywhere. Pick by experiment.
  • Limitations of word2vec/GloVe: they ignore morphology (dog vs dogs), handle rare/unseen words badly, aren't grounded in facts, aren't aligned across languages, aren't interpretable, encode bias, and are static.
  • Fixes: FastText (character n-grams), subword tokenizers (BPE, WordPiece), multilingual alignment, debiasing, contextual embeddings.

How do we know an embedding is any good?

Intrinsic

Evaluate on a specific intermediate sub-task. ✔ Fast to compute, ✔ helps you understand the method. ✘ Unclear whether it helps a real task until the correlation is shown.

Extrinsic

Evaluate on a real downstream task. ✔ Shows real usefulness. ✘ Can take a long time (a whole new dataset and model to train).

Intrinsic evaluation: four tests

TestWhat it checksExample
Relatedness / similarityDoes cosine similarity agree with human similarity scores? (correlation)Humans rate car–automobile ≈ 0.95, and cosine should be high too
AnalogyDoes vector arithmetic find the missing word?man : king :: woman : ? → queen (Google analogy set, BATS)
CategorisationDo same-category words cluster? (purity / F-score){dog, cat, horse} vs {apple, orange, banana}
Selectional preferenceDo typical verb–noun pairs score higher?"eat an apple" ≫ "eat a rock"
Still used today: probing LLM layers

Big models have dozens of layers, and we don't really know what each one does. Researchers pull out the representations from each layer and test them on small tasks like these (e.g. POS tagging). If layer 2's representations do badly at POS tagging, layer 2 probably isn't encoding POS. This was an early form of interpretability; today there are fancier tools like sparse autoencoders and circuit tracing.

Extrinsic evaluation: plug into a real task

Downstream tasks: question answering, sentiment analysis, NER, machine translation. These are well-defined problems you could ship to users. You measure with the task's metrics (F1 for classification, BLEU for translation), not with similarity scores.

StrategyWhat happensProsCons
Fixed (frozen, pre-trained)Embeddings are not updated while training the taskFewer parameters, less overfittingCan't adapt to task-specific meanings
Fine-tunedEmbeddings are updated along with the task (the objective is the task, e.g. translation)Task-adaptiveRisk of forgetting general knowledge
CombinedConcatenate pre-trained and learned embeddingsGeneral + task-specific informationBigger model, more computation
Which embedding should I use?

A comparison paper tested many embeddings (SGNS, GloVe, PPMI, SVD…) on relatedness, categorisation and analogy, and the winner changed from task to task. No one size fits all. Try them and keep what scores best.

What's wrong with word2vec and GloVe?

1. Blind to word structure (morphology)

dog and dogs, walk and walking can get unrelated vectors, because these models work at the whole-word level and ignore suffixes like -s and -ing. Rare words get poor vectors, and unseen (out-of-vocabulary) words get none at all.

Fix: FastText (Facebook AI Research)

Represent a word as the sum/average of its character n-gram vectors. With trigrams and boundary markers:

playing → <pl, pla, lay, ayi, yin, ing, ng>

Now playing, played and player share pieces like "pla" and "lay". And an unseen word such as "unknown" can still get a vector built from known pieces like "kno" and "own". Character vectors can also be fed through an LSTM to build a word vector.

Subword embeddings follow the same idea: modern tokenizers like Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) and WordPiece split words into frequent pieces. Morpheme-based versions split unfortunately → un + fortunate + ly, so unfortunate and fortunately share the root fortunate.

2. Not grounded in knowledge

Embeddings capture statistical patterns, not facts. Whale ends up close to fish because both appear near ocean, but a whale is not a fish. A model can wrongly treat similarity as fact.

3. Not aligned across languages

English dog and Spanish perro are trained in separate spaces, so they don't line up. Multilingual alignment methods learn a transformation (e.g. a rotation matrix) that maps one language's space onto another's so that cat lands near 猫 (māo). Some methods need no bilingual dictionary at all. The broader lesson: linear algebra matters, and research increasingly works in these latent spaces.

4. Not interpretable

In a 300-dimensional vector, what does dimension 17 = 2.1 mean? Nobody knows.

5. They encode bias

Example

Analogy tests produced man : computer programmer :: woman : homemaker. The vectors learned this from human-written text. Mitigations: post-processing / debiasing, and training on balanced or filtered corpora (often curated by hand). No large model today is fully unbiased. As the instructor put it, models can't be unbiased while the humans writing the data aren't.

6. Static: one vector per word

"bank" gets the same vector in "river bank" and "bank loan". Contextual embeddings (e.g. BERT, built on transformers) give a different vector for each usage.

From the lecture slides

  • Intrinsic relatedness metric: Spearman or Pearson correlation between cosine similarity and human scores (WordSim-353, SimLex-999). E.g. human car–automobile = 0.95 vs embedding cosine ≈ 0.92 means high correlation, a good model. Analogy datasets: Google Analogy Test Set, BATS. Categorisation: k-means clusters scored by purity or F-score.
  • Extrinsic caveat: it's unclear whether the subsystem or its interaction with other parts is the problem. "If replacing exactly one subsystem with another improves accuracy, winning!" Initialising with pre-trained embeddings gives faster convergence and better generalisation with little data.
  • Character-based embeddings: "cats" → c a t s, each character vector fed through a BiLSTM or CNN to compose a word vector. Unseen words like "catsy" still get vectors.
  • Character n-grams of length 3–6 for <where> (plus the whole word): n = 3: <wh, whe, her, ere, re> · n = 4: <whe, wher, here, ere> · n = 5: <wher, where, here> · n = 6: <where, where>.
  • FastText (Facebook AI Research, 2016): vword = Σg∈ngrams(word) zg. Training is still skip-gram/CBOW, but the updates go to all the sub-word vectors (input: n-grams of "playing", output: context words like "football", "he").
  • Unsupervised cross-lingual alignment: Artetxe et al. 2017 use identical strings (numbers, names, "COVID", "2025", "UN") as anchors, learn a linear map W from X to Y, and refine with mutual nearest neighbours. Zhang et al. 2017 use adversarial training: a generator learns W while a discriminator guesses the language, until the two spaces are indistinguishable. Supervised alignment: Faruqui et al. 2014.
  • Sparse embeddings for interpretability: add an L1 penalty (λ controls sparsity) so most dimensions are zero and each active one tends to mean something ("is animal", "is location").
  • De-biasing (Bolukbasi et al. 2016): (1) pick bias-defining pairs (he/she…), (2) compute the bias direction, (3) neutralise words that should be neutral. Perfect debiasing remains hard.

Practice

P1. List the character trigrams (with < >) of "dogs" and "dog". How many do they share?

dog: <do, dog, og> · dogs: <do, dog, ogs, gs>. They share 2 (<do, dog), so their vectors end up similar.

P2. With boundary markers, how many trigrams does a word of length L have? Check with "playing".

The padded length is L + 2, so there are L + 2 − 3 + 1 = L trigrams. "playing" (7 letters) has 7: <pl, pla, lay, ayi, yin, ing, ng>.

P3. Human similarity scores for 3 word pairs: 9, 5, 1. Model cosines: 0.8, 0.6, 0.1. Is this good intrinsically?

Yes. The ranking is identical (pair 1 > pair 2 > pair 3), so the rank correlation is perfect (Spearman = 1).

P4 (slide-style). List all 4-grams of <where>, and the total number of 3–6-grams (excluding the whole word).

4-grams: <whe, wher, here, ere> (4). Counts: n=3: 5, n=4: 4, n=5: 3, n=6: 2, so 14 n-grams (+ the word "<where>" itself).
