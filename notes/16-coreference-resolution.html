16. Coreference Resolution

Finding every mention of the same entity: mention-pair, mention-ranking, end-to-end and clustering models, plus B-Cubed scoring.

Part B · Neural NLP🎙 Neural NLP slides FOVL4 (Nov)

In 30 seconds

  • Coreference resolution finds all mentions that refer to the same real-world entity ("Barack Obama … He … his").
  • Two steps: detect mentions (relatively easy, though mentions can be nested), then cluster them (hard).
  • Anaphora points backwards ("John … he"); cataphora points forwards ("Before she arrived, Anna…").
  • Three model families: mention-pair (score every pair, threshold, transitive closure), mention-ranking (each mention picks its single best antecedent, possibly a dummy NA), and clustering (merge clusters greedily).
  • Scoring has evolved from hand features → neural features → the end-to-end span model (Lee et al. 2017).
  • Evaluate clusters with B-Cubed precision and recall, computed per mention and averaged.

What is coreference?

Example (slide)

Barack Obama nominated Hillary Rodham Clinton as his secretary of state on Monday. He chose her because she had foreign affairs experience as a former First Lady.

Clusters: {Barack Obama, his, He}, {Hillary Rodham Clinton, secretary of state, her, she, First Lady}, …

Applications: full text understanding (information extraction, QA, summarisation: "He was born in 1961" — who?), machine translation (languages mark gender and number differently), and dialogue systems ("Book tickets to see James Bond" → "Spectre is playing near you at 2:00 and 3:00 today. How many tickets would you like?" → "Two tickets for the showing at three").

It's really difficult, because it needs world knowledge and reasoning.

Step 1: mention detection

A mention is a span of text referring to some entity: pronouns (I, your, it, she), named entities (people, places…), and noun phrases ("a dog", "the big fluffy cat stuck in the tree"). Use a POS tagger, an NER system and a parser respectively.

Two steps on the slide example

Detect: "[I] voted for [Nader] because [he] was most aligned with [[my] values]," [she] said. Mentions can be nested ("my" inside "my values").
Cluster: {I, my}, {Nader, he}, {she}.

Not so simple: marking every pronoun, NE and NP over-generates. Are "It is sunny", "Every student", "No student", "The best donut in the world", "100 miles" mentions? There's a grey area, so pick a convention. Handling bad mentions: train a classifier to filter spurious ones, or (more common) keep them all as candidates and discard singletons (mentions linked to nothing) at the end.

Some linguistics: anaphora, coreference and cataphora

Coreference

Two mentions refer to the same entity in the world: "Barack Obama" … "Obama".

Anaphora

A term (the anaphor) refers back to an earlier one (the antecedent), and its interpretation depends on it: "Barack Obama said he…". Also: "John arrived late because he missed the bus", "I bought a new laptop. It is very fast." Most English pronoun references are anaphoric.

Cataphora refers forward: "Before she arrived, Anna phoned to say she'd be late." "If you need one, there's a pen on the table." It's less common, typical of literary style.

Model 1: mention pair

Train a binary classifier giving p(mi, mj) = probability the two mentions corefer. For the example: positive pairs (I, my), (Nader, he); negatives like (I, Nader), (she, my).

J = − Σi=2..N Σj=1..i yij log p(mj, mi) ,   yij = 1 if coreferent, −1 otherwiseiterate through mentions i and candidate antecedents j (earlier mentions). Ordinary binary cross-entropy.

Test time: pick a threshold (e.g. 0.5), add a coreference link where p > threshold, then take the transitive closure: if A–B and B–C, then A–C. This builds clusters automatically.

Disadvantages: one missing link can fragment a cluster; one wrong high score (say p(Nader, I) = 0.7) can merge everything into a giant cluster ("chain explosion"). Also, many mentions have only one clear antecedent, yet we ask the model to predict all of them. That motivates ranking models.

Model 2: mention ranking

Assign each mention its single highest-scoring antecedent. A dummy NA antecedent lets the model decline to link (for a first mention or a singleton). Apply a softmax over each mention's candidate scores so they sum to 1.

maximise   Σj=1..i−1 𝟙(yij = 1) · p(mj, mi)the model can put 0.9 on any one correct antecedent and still score well

At test time it's like mention-pair, except each mention gets exactly one antecedent: my → I, he → Nader, she → NA (or her own speaker).

How are the probabilities computed?

1. Non-neural, feature-based

Features for a pair, e.g. ("he", "Nader"): gender_match = 1, number_match = 1, animacy_match = 1, exact_string_match = 0, head_match = 0, is_pronoun_m = 1, is_pronoun_a = 0, sentence_distance = 0, mention_distance = 1, same_NE_type = 1, person_match = 1 … fed into a classifier. (gender_match = 1 if gender(mᵢ) = gender(aⱼ), else 0.)

2. Neural (feed-forward) model

Input: embeddings of the candidate antecedent and mention (head word, previous words…), distance, document genre, speaker info, and head-word match. The head word comes from a dependency or constituency parser: "The fluffy cat stuck in the tree" → cat.

3. End-to-end model (Lee et al. 2017)

  1. Embed words with a word-embedding matrix and a character-level CNN.
  2. Run a bidirectional LSTM over the document.
  3. Represent every span i (START(i)…END(i)), e.g. "the postal service", as gᵢ = [x*START(i), x*END(i), x̂ᵢ, φ(i)]: the boundary hidden states (context left and right), an attention-weighted average of the span's words x̂ᵢ (the span itself, a learned "head"), and extra features φ(i) (e.g. span length).
  4. Attention score αt = wα · FFNNα(x*t), softmaxed over the span's words.
  5. Score every pair of spans: s(i, j) = sm(i) + sm(j) + sa(i, j) ("is i a mention?" + "is j a mention?" + "do they corefer?").

Scoring every pair is intractable: O(T²) spans means O(T⁴) runtime, so aggressive pruning keeps only likely mentions. Attention learns which words are important, e.g. "(A fire in a Bangladeshi garment factory)" … "(the blaze)".

Model 3: clustering-based

  1. Start with every mention in its own cluster.
  2. Score possible merges s(c₁, c₂) with a neural model.
  3. Merge the best-scoring pair, and repeat until no score is positive (agglomerative clustering).

Cluster-pair score: take all mention pairs across the two clusters → a vector for each pair (like the mention-pair hidden layer) → pool (max/average) → score with a learned weight vector. Easier than pairwise linking because a cluster provides many signals at once.

Evaluation: B-Cubed

P(m) = |system cluster(m) ∩ gold cluster(m)| / |system cluster(m)|     R(m) = |system cluster(m) ∩ gold cluster(m)| / |gold cluster(m)|compute per mention, then average over all mentions. Over-merging lowers precision; under-merging lowers recall.

The intended clusters after transitive closure are {I, my}, {Nader, he}, {she}. But note: since (she, I) = 0.85 > 0.5, closure would actually give {I, my, she}. This is exactly the kind of error the slides warn about. Similarly, if the model wrongly links my ↔ she, closure forces I ↔ she.

C2. Mention ranking: best antecedent for "she"

p(NA, she) = 0.1, p(I, she) = 0.5, p(Nader, she) = 0.1, p(he, she) = 0.1, p(my, she) = 0.2. These sum to 1 after softmax. Best antecedent = I (0.5).

C3. Clustering merges

"Google announced Google Plus, and the company said the product will launch soon." C1 = {Google}, C2 = {Google Plus}, C3 = {the company}, C4 = {the product}.
Score(Google, the company) = 5 → merge · Score(Google Plus, the product) = 4 → merge · Score(Google, Google Plus) = −3 → don't merge.
Result: {Google, the company}, {Google Plus, the product}. To score merging these two, look at the 4 cross pairs: (Google, Google Plus), (Google, the product), (the company, Google Plus), (the company, the product).

C4. B-Cubed precision (slide)

System: [A B C D E], [F G H I]. Gold: [A B C D F G], [E H I]. 9 mentions.

A: system {A B C D E}, gold {A B C D F G}, overlap 4 → P = 4/5, R = 4/6. (Same for B, C, D.)
E: system {A B C D E}, gold {E H I}, overlap 1 → P = 1/5, R = 1/3.
F, G, H, I: each overlaps 2 of the 4 in its system cluster → P = 2/4.

P = [4(4/5) + 1(1/5) + 2(2/4) + 2(2/4)] / 9 = (3.2 + 0.2 + 1 + 1)/9 = 5.4/9 = 0.6

Practice Same level

P1. Finish the B-Cubed example: compute B-Cubed recall.

A–D: 4/6 each → 4 × 0.667 = 2.667. E: 1/3 = 0.333. F, G (gold {A B C D F G}, overlap {F, G}): 2/6 each → 0.667. H, I (gold {E H I}, overlap {H, I}): 2/3 each → 1.333. R = (2.667 + 0.333 + 0.667 + 1.333)/9 = 5/9 ≈ 0.556. F1 = 2(0.6)(0.556)/(1.156) ≈ 0.577.

P2. System [A B C], [D E]; gold [A B], [C D E]. B-Cubed precision?

A: system {A,B,C} ∩ gold {A,B} = 2 → 2/3. B: 2/3. C: {A,B,C} ∩ {C,D,E} = 1 → 1/3. D: {D,E} ∩ {C,D,E} = 2 → 2/2. E: 1. P = (2/3 + 2/3 + 1/3 + 1 + 1)/5 = 3.667/5 ≈ 0.733.

P3. Mention-pair scores with threshold 0.6: (A,B) = 0.9, (B,C) = 0.7, (A,C) = 0.2, (C,D) = 0.4. Final clusters?

Links: A–B, B–C. Transitive closure gives {A, B, C} and {D}, even though (A, C) alone was only 0.2. That's the chain effect.

P4. Mention ranking for "he": p(NA) = 0.05, p(I) = 0.05, p(Nader) = 0.85, p(my) = 0.05. Antecedent?

Nader, giving the cluster {Nader, he}.

P5. A document has T = 100 words. Roughly how many spans, and how many span pairs?

Spans ≈ T(T+1)/2 = 5,050 (O(T²)). Pairs ≈ 5,050²/2 ≈ 12.75 million (O(T⁴)). Hence pruning.

P6. Anaphora or cataphora? (a) "When he woke up, Ravi made tea." (b) "Priya lost her keys."

(a) Cataphora ("he" points forward to Ravi). (b) Anaphora ("her" points back to Priya).

Exam tip

Know the two steps, anaphora vs cataphora, how mention-pair (threshold + transitive closure) differs from mention-ranking (softmax, NA), the end-to-end span representation gᵢ, and how to compute B-Cubed precision and recall by hand.
