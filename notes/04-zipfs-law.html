04. Zipf's Law: The Shape of Language

Why 'the' is everywhere and most words are rare — and what that says about human effort.

Part A · Classical NLP🎙 Language Lecture 4a

In 30 seconds

  • Heights and exam marks follow a bell curve. Word frequencies don't.
  • Zipf's law: rank words from most to least frequent. Then frequency × rank ≈ constant.
  • So the #1 word appears about twice as often as #2, three times as often as #3, and so on. On a log–log plot it's a straight line with slope ≈ −1.
  • A few short function words (the, of, and) are very frequent. Most words are rare (the long tail).
  • Why? The principle of least effort, i.e. a balance between efficiency (short, easy words) and robustness (long, clear, informative words).

The question: are words "normally" distributed?

Many things in nature follow the normal distribution (bell curve): most values sit in the middle and a few are extreme.

Example: rolling three dice

Roll three dice and add them. The minimum is 1+1+1 = 3 and the maximum is 6+6+6 = 18. Do it thousands of times and the totals pile up around 10–11, with very few 3s or 18s. That's a bell curve. Life expectancy, marks in a class of 100, and children's heights look similar.

So you'd expect word frequencies to behave the same way, with most words appearing an "average" number of times. They don't.

Zipf's discovery

In the early 1930s, American linguist George Kingsley Zipf gave each of his research assistants a different book. Their job: list every unique word and count how often it appears (by hand, with no computers). A French stenographer, Jean-Baptiste Estoup, had noticed a similar pattern around 1912. Zipf built on it ("standing on the shoulders of giants") and later applied the same law to city sizes and other human behaviour.

How to draw a Zipf plot

  1. Take a corpus and count each unique word.
  2. Sort the counts from highest to lowest.
  3. Give the most frequent word rank 1, the next rank 2, and so on (like class toppers: highest marks = rank 1).
  4. Plot frequency (y-axis) against rank (x-axis).
Zipf's law
f × r = k     (equivalently f ∝ 1 / r)f = frequency of a word, r = its rank, k = a constant for that corpus
Worked example: "the most frequent word appears twice as often as the second"

Words w1 (rank 1) and w2 (rank 2):

f1 × 1 = k   and   f2 × 2 = k   ⇒   f1 = 2 f2

If "the" appears 60,000 times, Zipf predicts rank 2 ≈ 30,000, rank 3 ≈ 20,000, rank 10 ≈ 6,000, rank 100 ≈ 600.

Real data: in the ~1-million-word Brown corpus, the ≈ 69,971 (about 7% of all words), of ≈ 36,411, and ≈ 28,852. Roughly, but not exactly, halving. Zipf gives rough estimates. Mandelbrot later refined the formula.

Class question (exam style)

Should the 50th most common word occur three times as often as the 150th?
Yes. f50 × 50 = f150 × 150, so f50 = 3 f150.

What the curve tells us

  • Very uneven distribution. A handful of words are extremely common, and a huge number appear once or twice (the "Zipfian tail"). So no, most words do not appear with average frequency.
  • The frequent words are function words: the, of, and, to, a, in. They're grammatical glue that joins content words (nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs).
  • Frequent words are short. Rare words are long. Compare "the" with "antidisestablishmentarianism".
  • It holds across languages. Piantadosi's cross-language study found roughly the same straight line on log–log plots, with small language-specific variations in slope.

Why does language look like this?

The principle of least effort (Zipf)

Who makes a word frequent? We do, by using it a lot. If we use a word constantly, we want it to cost little time and energy, so we keep it short.

Analogy: speed-dial

You put your most-called contacts on speed-dial (one tap) and leave rare contacts in the full list (scroll and search). Language does the same: everyday words get short "speed-dial" forms.

Efficiency vs robustness (an information-theory view)

Short words → efficient

Less effort, faster to say. But easy to mishear in noise, and since they're predictable they carry little information.

Long words → robust

Carry more information and can be recovered even if part is lost in noise. But they cost more mental and physical effort.

Key idea

Language seems to balance efficiency (minimise effort) and robustness (get the message across despite noise and distraction). That's a transmitter–noisy channel–receiver picture, which leads straight to Shannon in the next topic.

A student's good point: context and register

"In a professional email I can't write gotcha instead of got it." True. Word choice depends on context, and that's why we also look at pairs of words. Plot bigram frequencies ("John eats", "eats a", "a mango") and you'll find a Zipf-like pattern too.

The "Zipf mystery": the monkey at a typewriter

Give a monkey a keyboard with 26 letters + 1 space bar, every key equally likely (1/27). It types random characters, and a "word" is whatever falls between spaces. Collect a million such "words" and plot the frequencies. Do you get Zipf?

What the class found

Students who coded it found that random text also roughly follows Zipf's law. Short random strings are naturally more likely than long ones, and the number of possible words grows exponentially with length.

So is Zipf special to human language? Partly an open question. Human language is not random (you can predict the next word, which you can't do with monkey text), but the Zipf shape alone can come from simple statistics. That's why it's called a mystery.

Try it in Python: Zipf on any text + the monkey experiment 
import random, collections

def zipf_table(words, top=10):
    counts = collections.Counter(words).most_common()
    for rank, (w, f) in enumerate(counts[:top], start=1):
        print(f"{rank:>3} {w:<12} f={f:<7} f*r={f*rank}")

# 1) Your own corpus (any .txt file, e.g. a Hindi or English novel)
text = open("book.txt", encoding="utf-8").read().lower().split()
zipf_table(text)

# 2) Monkey typing: 26 letters + space, all equally likely
alphabet = "abcdefghijklmnopqrstuvwxyz "
monkey = "".join(random.choice(alphabet) for _ in range(5_000_000)).split()
zipf_table(monkey)

If f×r stays roughly constant down the list, Zipf holds. Try it on Hindi, Bengali, Telugu, whatever you speak, as the professor suggested.

Exam tip

Know the formula f·r = k, be able to derive "rank 1 is twice rank 2", explain why function words are short and frequent (least effort, efficiency vs robustness), and describe what the log–log plot looks like.

The slides frame this topic with one question: "How can we computationally represent the structure of language?" There are two answers: describe grammatical rules (subjects, verbs, objects…), or describe its statistical structure (how often words occur, how predictable one word is from another, how much information a word carries). Zipf's law is the first statistical answer. The "benefits" slide adds that Zipf's law helps us understand language structure and guides us to design better NLP systems.

Numericals

From class Worked in the lecture

C1. Range of the sum of three dice

Minimum 1+1+1 = 3, maximum 6+6+6 = 18. Rolled many times, the sums form a bell curve centred around 10.5. That was the example of a normal distribution that word frequencies do not follow.

C2. Show the most frequent word occurs twice as often as the second

f·r = k. Rank 1: f₁·1 = k. Rank 2: f₂·2 = k. Equate: f₁ = 2f₂.

C3. Does the 50th word occur 3× as often as the 150th?

f₅₀·50 = f₁₅₀·150, so f₅₀ = 3·f₁₅₀. Yes.

Practice Same level

P1. "the" (rank 1) appears 60,000 times. Predict the frequency of the rank-4 word, and the rank of a word seen 100 times.

k = 60,000 × 1 = 60,000. f₄ = 60,000/4 = 15,000. Rank of a word with f = 100: r = k/f = 600.

P2. The rank-5 word occurs 1,200 times. How often does the rank-20 word occur?

k = 5 × 1,200 = 6,000, so f₂₀ = 6,000/20 = 300. (Or directly: 4× the rank means ¼ the frequency.)

P3. Counts for ranks 1–4 are 1000, 480, 350, 240. Does this corpus follow Zipf?

f×r = 1000, 960, 1050, 960, which is roughly constant (≈1000). Yes, approximately.

P4. On a log₁₀–log₁₀ plot with k = 10⁵, what is log f at rank 1000? What is the slope?

log f = log k − log r = 5 − 3 = 2, so f = 100. The slope is −1.
