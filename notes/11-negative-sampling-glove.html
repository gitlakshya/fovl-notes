11. Negative Sampling & GloVe

Making word2vec fast, and learning meaning from co-occurrence ratios instead.

Part B · Neural NLP🎙 Neural NLP Lecture 3 (17 Oct)

In 30 seconds

  • Plain word2vec's softmax denominator sums over the whole vocabulary (100k+ words) for every training pair. Very slow.
  • Negative sampling: turn it into binary classification. Is (centre, context) a true pair or a random noise pair? Push true pairs together and noise pairs apart, using only K negatives.
  • Negatives are sampled from the unigram distribution raised to ¾, so "the, of, a" don't dominate.
  • Co-occurrence matrix: count once how often words appear near each other instead of sliding windows forever. It's sparse, so reduce it (SVD) or learn from it.
  • GloVe: meaning lives in ratios of co-occurrence probabilities. Train vectors so that wi·w̃j + biases ≈ log Xij.

The problem: an expensive denominator

P(o|c) = exp(uo·vc) / Σw∈V exp(uw·vc)   ← one term per vocabulary word, for every training pair

With 100,000+ words and billions of training pairs, that's far too slow.

Negative sampling

Key idea

Instead of "which of 100,000 words is the context?", ask a cheap yes/no question: "Is this a real (centre, context) pair or a fake one?" Train a binary logistic regression with one positive pair and K random negative pairs.

Example: "the cat sat on the mat"

Centre word cat. Positive (real) context words: sat, on. Negative samples (random words from the vocabulary): apple, car, quickly.

Training pulls cat ↔ sat closer and pushes cat ↔ apple apart.

Jneg(o, c) = − log σ(uo·vc) − Σk=1..K log σ(− uk·vc)
  • First term: σ(uo·vc) should be close to 1 for the true pair (e.g. king & queen co-occur, so their dot product grows).
  • Second term: for each of the K negatives, σ(−uk·vc) should be close to 1, i.e. their dot product with the centre should be small/negative (king vs table, banana, car).
  • Minimising J (= maximising the log-probabilities) does both at once. The sigmoid just turns dot products into probabilities between 0 and 1.

It does the same job as full word2vec (learning semantic relationships). It's an optimisation that avoids comparing against the entire vocabulary. In theory the clusters should look similar, and training is much faster.

Which words to pick as negatives?

If we sample from the raw word-frequency (unigram) distribution U(w), we'd nearly always pick the, of, a, is. So we reshape it:

Pneg(w) ∝ U(w)3/4
Worked example: what the ¾ power does

Three words with unigram probabilities 0.90, 0.09, 0.01.
Raise to ¾: 0.924, 0.164, 0.032 → normalise: 0.825, 0.147, 0.028.

The very common word drops from 90% to 82.5%, and the rare word nearly triples (1% → 2.8%). Rare words get picked more often.

(In practice also do pre-processing, e.g. removing stop words, so the model doesn't spend its effort on them.)

Exam question (practised in class): counting sigmoid computations

Skip-gram with negative sampling: vocabulary 10,000, d = 300, window = 2 (two words each side), K = 10 negatives. How many sigmoid computations for one occurrence of a target word (full window used)?

Context words = 2 left + 2 right = 4. For each context word: 1 positive + 10 negatives = 11 sigmoids.
Total = 4 × 11 = 44.

Co-occurrence matrices: count once, use everywhere

Sliding a window through the whole corpus again and again is wasteful. Why not accumulate the statistics once: how often does each word appear near each other word?

Worked example: window = 1

Corpus: "I like deep learning." "I like NLP." "I enjoy flying."

"I like" appears in two sentences, so the count is 2. Each row is now a vector for that word.

Problems: the matrix grows with vocabulary size (V × V), is very sparse, and is hard to store. Same old one-hot problems. Fix: dimensionality reduction such as SVD (X = U Σ Vᵀ, keep the top 25–1000 dimensions). That helps, but works less well than training vectors directly. Hence GloVe.

GloVe (Pennington, Socher & Manning, 2014)

GloVe = Global Vectors. Combines the global counts of co-occurrence matrices with the learning of word2vec. People found GloVe particularly helpful in tasks like sentiment analysis.

The insight: ratios carry meaning

How can a machine learn that ice relates to solid and steam to gas, without a dictionary? Look at ratios of co-occurrence probabilities, P(k | ice) / P(k | steam):

probe word ksolidgaswaterfashion
P(k | ice)1.9×10⁻⁴6.6×10⁻⁵3.0×10⁻³1.7×10⁻⁵
P(k | steam)2.2×10⁻⁵7.8×10⁻⁴2.2×10⁻³1.8×10⁻⁵
ratio8.9 ≫ 10.085 ≪ 11.36 ≈ 10.96 ≈ 1
  • Ratio ≫ 1: k is linked to ice (solid).
  • Ratio ≪ 1: k is linked to steam (gas).
  • Ratio ≈ 1: k doesn't tell them apart (water relates to both, fashion to neither).

Here P(k | i) = Xik / Xi: the number of times k appears near i, divided by all of i's co-occurrences.

From ratios to vectors

GloVe wants a function of the word vectors that reproduces these ratios. Meaning differences are vector differences (remember king − man + woman), so assume F depends on (wi − wj)·w̃k. Choosing F = exp and taking logs gives:

wi · w̃j + bi + b̃j ≈ log Xij

i.e. the dot product of two word vectors should match the log of how often they co-occur. The training loss is a weighted squared error:

J = Σi,j f(Xij) · ( wi·w̃j + bi + b̃j − log Xij )²

The weighting f(x): 0 when x = 0 (ignore pairs that never co-occur), grows for small counts, then levels off for very large counts so frequent pairs like (the, of) don't dominate. In the paper: f(x) = (x / xmax)¾ for x < xmax = 100, else 1.

GloVe in 6 steps
  1. Collect a corpus.
  2. Choose a context window.
  3. Build the co-occurrence matrix X.
  4. Give each word two vectors (w and w̃) plus biases.
  5. Predict log Xij with wi·w̃j + bi + b̃j; weight errors with f.
  6. After training, use wi (or wi + w̃i) as the embedding.

word2vec asks…

"How similar is this centre word to that context word?" (predictive, local windows)

GloVe asks…

"How much more likely is word k to appear with i than with j?" (count-based, global statistics)

Exam tip

Understand the negative-sampling loss term by term, why ¾ is used, how to build a co-occurrence matrix by hand, the ice/steam ratio intuition, and the GloVe objective with its weighting function. Practise the "count the sigmoids" style of question.

Try it

Add negative sampling to your skip-gram code (a sample_negatives(k) function plus the extra loss term inside the training loop). Then compare the 2-D plot of embeddings and the training time with and without it.

From the lecture slides

  • Objective as written on the slide (maximise): Jt(θ) = log σ(uoᵀvc) + Σj=1..k Ej∼P(w)[log σ(−ujᵀvc)], and J(θ) = (1/T) Σt Jt(θ). Here k = number of negatives, uj = output vector of the j-th negative, P(w) = noise distribution, E = average over sampled negatives. The minimisation form is the same with a minus sign.
  • Intuition slide: positive pair (king, queen) → log σ(uqueenᵀvking) is pushed up. Negatives (king, table), (king, banana), (king, car) → log σ(−utableᵀvking) etc. are pushed up, i.e. those dot products are pushed down. "SGNS learns embeddings by maximising the similarity of real pairs and minimising the similarity of random pairs, using the sigmoid instead of the expensive softmax."
  • Two kinds of co-occurrence matrix: window-based (like word2vec, captures syntactic and semantic information: "word space") vs word–document (gives general topics, e.g. all sports terms look alike, which leads to Latent Semantic Analysis: "document space"). Typical window length is 5–10. The class example used 1, and it's symmetric.
  • SVD hacks (Rohde et al. 2005, COALS), because SVD on raw counts works poorly: log the frequencies; cap with min(X, t), t ≈ 100; ignore function words; use ramped windows (count closer words more); use Pearson correlations instead of counts and set negatives to 0.
  • GloVe derivation steps (slides 36–42): Pik = P(wk|wi) = Xik/Xi, Xi = Σk Xik → want F(wi, wj, w̃k) = Pik/Pjk → three assumptions: (1) only the difference wi − wj matters, (2) the relation should be linear, (3) the output is a scalar → f = (wi − wj)ᵀw̃k → wrap it in exp to keep it positive → take logs: log Pik = wiᵀw̃k + bi + b̃k.
  • GloVe algorithm (slide pseudocode): when building X, add 1/distance for each context word (closer words count more). Train only on pairs with X[i, j] > 0: target = log X[i, j], prediction = w[i]·w̃[j] + b[i] + b̃[j], loss = f(X[i, j])·(prediction − target)². Final embedding W[i] = w[i] + w̃[i] (or their average, or w[i] alone).

Practice Same level

P1. Window 1 (one word each side), K = 15. Sigmoids per target occurrence? And with window 3, K = 5?

2 × (1 + 15) = 32. 6 × (1 + 5) = 36.

P2. Negative-sampling loss: uo·vc = 2, and two negatives with dot products 1 and −1. Compute J (natural log).

J = −ln σ(2) − ln σ(−1) − ln σ(1) = 0.127 + 1.313 + 0.313 ≈ 1.75. The negative with dot product +1 (too similar) contributes the most, so training pushes it away.

P3. Build the window-1 co-occurrence counts for "the cat sat. the cat ran."

X(the, cat) = 2, X(cat, sat) = 1, X(cat, ran) = 1, X(sat, .) = 1, X(ran, .) = 1 (symmetric matrix). The pair "the cat" dominates.

P4. P(k|i) = 0.02 and P(k|j) = 0.001. Interpret the ratio. If Xij = 20, what should wi·w̃j + bi + b̃j be close to?

Ratio = 20 ≫ 1, so k is associated with i. Target = ln 20 ≈ 3.0.

P5. Unigram probabilities 0.6, 0.3, 0.1. Negative-sampling probabilities after the ¾ power?

0.60.75 = 0.682, 0.30.75 = 0.405, 0.10.75 = 0.178 → sum 1.265 → 0.539, 0.320, 0.141. The rare word rises from 10% to 14%.

P6. Window 2 with 1/distance weighting: what does centre "deep" add for "I like deep learning"?

X[deep, like] += 1, X[deep, learning] += 1, X[deep, I] += 1/2.
