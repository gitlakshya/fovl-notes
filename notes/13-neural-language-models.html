13. From N-grams to Neural Language Models

Count-based LMs, text generation, and the fixed-window neural LM.

Part B · Neural NLP🎙 Neural NLP Lectures 4–5 (19 & 23 Oct)

In 30 seconds

  • A language model predicts the next word. Do it repeatedly and you generate text.
  • Count-based n-gram LM: P(w | last n−1 words) = count ratio. Simple, but it ignores older context, suffers from sparsity, and needs huge storage.
  • Fixed-window neural LM (Bengio et al., 2003): concatenate the embeddings of the last few words → hidden layer → softmax over the vocabulary. No sparsity problem, no n-gram tables.
  • Its weaknesses: the window is always too small, a bigger window means a bigger W, and each position has its own weights (no sharing). That motivates RNNs.

Language modelling = next-word prediction

P( x(t+1) | x(t), …, x(1) )

Phone keyboards, search autocomplete and chatbots all do this.

Count-based (n-gram) language models, revisited

Before deep learning: collect statistics about how often different n-grams occur and use them to predict. Under the Markov assumption, x(t+1) depends only on the previous n−1 words.

Worked example: a 4-gram model

Sentence: "As the proctor started the clock, the students opened their ___". A 4-gram model throws away everything except the last 3 words:

P(w | students opened their) = count(students opened their w) / count(students opened their)

Suppose in the corpus "students opened their" occurred 1000 times, followed by "books" 400 times and "exams" 100 times:

P(books | …) = 400/1000 = 0.4     P(exams | …) = 100/1000 = 0.1

The model predicts books. But the discarded word proctor tells us it's an exam, so exams or answer sheets would fit better. Lesson: ignoring context hurts.

Problems with n-gram LMs

  • Sparsity (numerator): "students opened their petri-dishes" never seen → probability 0 → needs smoothing.
  • Sparsity (denominator): "students opened their" never seen → can't compute at all → back off to "opened their".
  • Storage: you have to store counts for every n-gram, and that grows with corpus size and n. Increasing n makes sparsity worse.

Generating text with an n-gram model

Example: trigram model trained on ~1.7M words of business/financial news

Start with "today the" and look at the next-word distribution: company 0.153, bank 0.153, price 0.077, italian 0.039, emirate 0.039, …
Sample one (say "price"), append it, condition on "the price", sample again, and so on:

today the price of gold per ton , while production of shoe lasts and shoe industry , the bank intervened just after it considered and rejected an imf demand …

Surprisingly grammatical in places, but incoherent overall. More data and a larger n help somewhat.

Analogy: a bag of words

Think of the corpus as a big bag of word slips: "cat" ×5, "orange" ×6, and so on. Probability = how likely you are to pull a given slip out. Counting is just measuring the bag.

A neural language model

We already know how to build a neural network (topic 09) and how to look up word vectors (topic 10). So: feed the context words in, multiply by weights, softmax over the vocabulary, and pick the next word.

Worked example: a 4-word vocabulary

Vocabulary: books, houses, lamps, stamps. The context ("students opened their") is turned into a hidden vector x (3-D here; often 100–300-D in practice). The output weight matrix is 4 × 3 (vocabulary × hidden size), so W x gives 4 scores:

W·x = [1.8, −2.2, 2.9, −0.3]   → softmax →   [0.24, 0.004, 0.73, 0.03]

Prediction: lamps (0.73). The gold answer was books, so the loss = −log 0.24 ≈ 1.43. Back-propagate the error and update the weights, which nudges P(books) up next time.

One recipe, many tasks

Change only what goes in and what you compare against. For sentiment analysis, the "vocabulary" of outputs is just {positive, negative}. For translation, the input is a sentence in language A and the target is the sentence in language B. Once the input/output setup is clear, you can adapt a neural network to almost any task.

The fixed-window neural LM (Bengio et al., 2000/2003)

The same window idea works for tagging: to label "Paris" as a LOCATION in "museums in Paris are amazing", feed in the window around it.

✔ Improvements over n-grams

No sparsity problem (every word has an embedding, so similar contexts share strength). No need to store every observed n-gram.

✘ Remaining problems

The window is too small. Enlarging it enlarges W. And x(1), x(2)… are multiplied by completely different parts of W: no parameter sharing, no symmetry.

Why "no parameter sharing" is bad

"The students opened their…" vs "Yesterday the students opened their…". The word "students" sits at position 2 in one and position 3 in the other, so it hits different weights. The model has to learn the same fact about "students" separately for each position. That wastes data and capacity and generalises poorly.

What we need: an architecture that can read any length of input and reuses the same weights at every position. That's the RNN (next topic).

Hands-on: an n-gram LM in PyTorch

  1. Load a real dataset through PyTorch/torchtext/datasets (e.g. IMDb, WikiText) instead of a hand-made list.
  2. Tokenize, build the vocabulary, convert to tensors, split train/test.
  3. Model: embedding → linear → softmax; train to predict the next word from the previous n−1 words.
  4. Report accuracy (correct predictions / total) and generate a paragraph: feed in "I like", take the most probable word, append it, repeat 50 times.
What students saw

Generated text like "my friend is loud guitar loud guitar loud guitar…". Repetition was a classic problem in pre-transformer models (the instructor recalled RNN outputs like "I do not know" or "thank you thank you…"). A model can only output words in its vocabulary, and a larger n (more context) improves fluency. Transformers changed things because they scale with more data. Long-document generation is still hard today.

Exam tip

Compute an n-gram probability from counts, list the problems (context, sparsity, storage), write the fixed-window neural LM equations, and explain "no parameter sharing" with an example.

From the lecture slides

  • An LM assigns a probability to text: P(x⁽¹⁾…x⁽ᵀ⁾) = Πt P(x⁽ᵗ⁾ | x⁽ᵗ⁻¹⁾…x⁽¹⁾), the chain rule. LMs are a benchmark task and a sub-component of predictive typing, speech and handwriting recognition, spelling/grammar correction, authorship identification, MT, summarisation and dialogue. "ChatGPT is an LM!"
  • What next-word prediction can teach (slide): trivia ("Stanford University is located in ___, California"), syntax ("I put ___ fork down"), coreference ("…checking for traffic over ___ shoulder"), lexical semantics ("fish, turtles, seals, and ___"), sentiment ("The movie was ___"), reasoning (Iroh/Zuko), arithmetic (1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21, ___).
  • n-gram definitions: unigrams "the", "students"…; bigrams "the students", "students opened"…; trigrams "the students opened"…; 4-gram "the students opened their". In practice n can't be much bigger than 5.
  • Ideal LM distribution: smooth, fine-grained, and able to distinguish between many reasonable next words. The trigram model's "company 0.153, bank 0.153" shows coarse granularity.
  • Composition functions (turning a prefix of word embeddings into one vector): element-wise sum, concatenation, feed-forward network, CNN, RNN, transformer.
  • Each row of W stores the weights that score how compatible the prefix is with one output word. That's why W has shape (vocab × input dim) = 4 × 3.

C3. Neural output layer (slides 19–23)

Vocabulary: books, houses, lamps, stamps. Target distribution <0.6, 0.2, 0.1, 0.1>. Prefix vector x = <−2.3, 0.9, 5.4>.

W = [[1.2, −0.3, 0.9], [0.2, 0.4, −2.2], [8.9, −1.9, 6.5], [4.5, 2.2, −0.1]]

Row 1: 1.2(−2.3) + (−0.3)(0.9) + 0.9(5.4) = −2.76 − 0.27 + 4.86 = 1.8. Likewise Wx = <1.8, −11.9, 12.9, −8.9>.
The slide then uses Wx = <1.8, −1.9, 2.9, −0.9> to show softmax(Wx) = <0.24, 0.006, 0.73, 0.02>, so "lamps" is predicted, which doesn't match the target (books 0.6). The loss −log 0.24 drives the update.

C4. Fixed-window model dimensions

Input: 4 one-hot words → concatenated embeddings e = [e⁽¹⁾; e⁽²⁾; e⁽³⁾; e⁽⁴⁾] → h = f(We + b₁) → ŷ = softmax(Uh + b₂).

P3. Using C2, what is the probability of generating "today the price of gold" given "today the"?

P(price|today the) × P(of|the price) × P(gold|price of) = 0.077 × 0.308 × 0.018 ≈ 0.00043.

P4. "the price of" occurs 500 times, followed by gold 150, oil 100. P(gold), P(oil)?

0.30 and 0.20.

P5. Fixed window of 4 words, 300-d embeddings, hidden 500, vocabulary 10,000. Size of W and U?

W: 1,200 × 500 = 600,000. U: 500 × 10,000 = 5,000,000. Doubling the window to 8 doubles W.
