01. What is Language?

Language as a window to the mind: symbols, meaning, the inner and outer world, and why language is slippery.

Part A · Classical NLP🎙 Language Lecture 1 (+ start of Lecture 2)

In 30 seconds

  • Language is a window to the mind: it turns messy private thoughts into organised, shareable symbols.
  • It has layers: sounds → words → sentences → meaning → use in context (phonology, morphology, syntax, semantics, pragmatics).
  • Every symbol is a form–meaning pair: the word "cat" isn't a cat, it's a key that unlocks your concept of a cat.
  • Language doesn't copy reality. It helps the listener rebuild it ("projected reality").
  • Because symbols are flexible, language is also ambiguous, and that's the central headache of NLP.

Why start with "what is language"?

We use language all day, so we take it for granted, the way a fish doesn't notice water. The professor's warning was a good one: a lot of AI today just pours text into models without asking how language actually works. If we understand the machine we're copying (the human mind), we'll design better algorithms. So before any maths, let's look at what language is.

Analogy: the messy room

Sit still, close your eyes, and watch your thoughts. They pop up randomly, half-formed and overlapping, like clothes thrown around a messy room. Language is the act of tidying the room: putting each idea in a labelled drawer and in a sensible order. Once things are organised, they start to mean something, to you and to anyone you show the room to.

So language does two jobs at once:

  1. It expresses thought. Idea → concept → you say it out loud using some medium (speech, writing, signs).
  2. It structures thought. Organising ideas is what gives them meaning.

The layers of language

Think of language as a building with floors. Each floor is built from the one below it.

Example: pragmatics in action

"I sat near the bank." A finance person pictures SBI; a nature lover pictures the bank of the Ganga. The words are the same, and only context settles it. Pragmatics studies exactly this: context, discourse, and how sentences fit together coherently.

The professor's Hindi example: if you've just been talking about India ("India is a great nation… it has many states…"), then "Bharat ke pradhan mantri Narendra Modi hain" (India's PM is Modi) sounds natural, while flipping the order to start with the name sounds odd. Both sentences are grammatical, but only one fits the flow. You'll find the same thing in Telugu, Malayalam, Kannada or any language you speak.

What do we use language for?

Talking to friends, shopping, bargaining, persuading. Language is also not the only way to communicate: there's sign language, Morse code, pictures, gestures, even Bluetooth between two phones. What makes human language special is how compact and effective it is.

Example: three words, a lot of meaning

"Let's meet tomorrow." In three words you've said when (not next month, tomorrow), shown urgency, and made a firm, assertive proposal. That's how efficient language is.

Communication needs two parties: a sender (encoder) who packs the idea into words, and a receiver (decoder) who unpacks it. Language does this through two kinds of function:

1. Symbolic function

Language labels concepts with symbols. Want to talk about a tree? Use the word tree. Something abstract like justice or freedom? There's a word for that too. Symbols combine into bigger ideas, and the thing doesn't need to be present: you can talk about a lion without one in the room.

2. Interactive function

Language is how we get things done with other people: persuading, explaining, asking. It's a "two-body problem": the speaker wants to save effort (less jaw movement, shorter sentences) but also wants the listener to understand. Every sentence is a small trade-off between the two.

Symbols, forms and meaning

Language expresses itself through symbols: letters, numbers, prefixes, words, phrases, whole sentences. Even a logical statement like "If it rains tomorrow, I won't go to class" is one big symbol for an if–then idea.

The concrete shape a symbol takes is its form: spoken, written, or signed. Like clay that can be moulded into many shapes, one idea can take many forms.

Key idea: form–meaning pairing

A form is only useful if it's paired with a meaning that others also accept. That pairing, form + meaning, is the basic unit of language. "Meaning" here means an idea your mind understands and that is conventionally accepted by the people you're talking to.

Example: the dollar sign

Why does $ make you think "money" when there's no money in front of you? Because you've learned the convention that this form pairs with that meaning. Same with ₹.

Inner world vs outer world

Language doesn't represent the outside world directly. It represents our conceptualised experience of it. There's an outer world (what's out there) and an inner world (what's in your head), and language is the bridge between them.

Example: the pear

Hold a pear. Different parts of your brain register its shape, colour, texture, taste and smell. These are percepts, raw signals. Your brain combines them into one mental image and matches it against a concept you already have: "pear". Only then does the concept get linked to a meaning and a form (the word, or a picture, or a gesture).

Perception vs meaning: you can perceive plenty of things you have no concept for. Without a concept, the signals are just noise. The concept is what connects perception to meaning.

Analogy: if you weren't allowed to say "fear"

The professor asked: you saw a dangerous animal and felt fear. Now you can't use fear, scared, afraid, or any similar word. What do you do? Students suggested the colour red, a skull symbol, gestures, or just keeping your distance. Point: one concept can be expressed through many different forms.

Projected reality: language rebuilds, it doesn't transmit

Key idea

Language doesn't send reality from my head to yours. It sends symbols that let you reconstruct a version of reality. This is called projected reality.

That's how we can talk about things that aren't in front of us, or don't exist at all. Grammar gives us tools for each kind of reality:

Type of realityLanguage toolExample
Presentpresent continuous (is …-ing)The cat is sleeping.
Pastpast tense (was)The cat was sleeping.
Imaginedordinary words in impossible combinationsA talking banana. A cat flying over the moon. A city on Mars.
Abstractabstract nouns, symbolsJustice is important. (Symbol for peace: a white dove.)

The catch: symbols can be ambiguous

The same flexibility that makes language powerful also makes it confusing.

Example 1: "The cat jumped over the wall"

Did the cat leap across to the other side? Jump up and land on top? The word over allows several readings, and jump says nothing about the shape of the path. Students drew different pictures for the same sentence.

Example 2: "John saw Mary with a telescope"

(a) John used a telescope to see Mary (two ships at sea). (b) John saw Mary, who happened to be carrying her new telescope. The culprit is the phrase "with a telescope", which can attach to John or to Mary. We'll meet this again as structural ambiguity in topics 03 and 08.

Bonus from Lecture 2: the Pirahã language

The class discussed the documentary on Pirahã, a language of the Amazon. Researchers argued it lacks recursion, the ability to nest a clause inside another clause.

What is recursion?

Main (matrix) clause with an embedded clause inside: "John said that Mary believed that Ram knew that…". You can keep nesting forever. Most languages allow this. Pirahã apparently doesn't. The speakers were also described as very "grounded": they talk about what they directly experience. This shows how much culture and language shape each other.

The professor also recommended a video on how a child acquires its first words, with a useful takeaway: when you train a computer on language, think of it as a child learning.

Exam tip

Documentaries won't be examined in depth, but basic factual MCQs (e.g. "which is true about Pirahã?") can appear. Be able to name the five levels of language with an example of each, and explain form–meaning pairing and projected reality.

Try it

Find a sentence in your mother tongue with two meanings. Which layer causes the ambiguity: a word (lexical) or the structure (structural)?

Open question from class

When can we say a machine has truly understood us? Language is a behaviour, and behaviour depends on both cognition and environment. Machines don't have human cognition, so we have to model it computationally. This is still an active research question, and it's the starting point of the next topic.
