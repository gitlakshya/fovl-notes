17. Question Answering Systems

Factoid vs complex QA, the IR-based pipeline, ranking, MRR and dense retrievers.

Part B · Neural NLP🎙 Neural NLP Lecture 7 (15 Nov, part 1)

In 30 seconds

  • QA was one of the first tests of whether computers "understand" language (TREC QA track, IBM Watson, Siri, Google Now, Wolfram Alpha).
  • Factoid questions have short factual answers ("Who wrote…?"). Complex / narrative questions need reasoning and long answers.
  • Two approaches: IR-based (search text, extract the answer) and knowledge-based (query a structured knowledge base), plus hybrids.
  • The IR pipeline: question processing → document/passage retrieval → answer extraction.
  • Evaluate with accuracy and Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR). Modern systems use dense retrievers (embeddings), which is the basis of today's RAG systems.

What QA looks like

Example: "What do worms eat?"

Candidate answers: "worms eat grass" ✔, "horses with worms eat grass" ✘, "birds eat worms" ✘, "grass is eaten by worms" ✔. Matching keywords isn't enough. The system has to understand who eats what.

  • IBM Watson won the Jeopardy! quiz show, where answers are hidden in tricky clues ("It's where Pele stores his ball" → soccer locker).
  • Siri was one of the first consumer spoken QA systems: "Do I need an umbrella tomorrow in San Francisco?" → "Yes, it should rain tomorrow."

Types of questions

Factoid

Short facts, usually a few words.
"Who wrote the Universal Declaration of Human Rights?"
"How many calories are in two slices of apple pie?"
"Where is the Louvre Museum located?" → Paris, France

Most commercial deep-learning QA systems target these.

Complex / narrative

Need reasoning and a long answer.
"In children with an acute febrile illness, what is the efficacy of acetaminophen in reducing fever?"

Web search already answers simple factoids ("Who wrote Harry Potter?"), but not questions that need deeper or contextual reasoning.

The IR-based pipeline, step by step

Step 1: Question processing (understand the question)

  • Answer type detection: what kind of entity is wanted? "Who founded Virgin Airlines?" → PERSON. "What Canadian city has the largest population?" → CITY. This narrows the search a lot.
  • Query formulation: pick the keywords for the search engine ("prime minister", "India").
  • Question type: definition? list? math?
  • Focus detection and relation extraction.
Example: structuring a question

"Which US state capital has the largest population?" becomes something like:
Answer type: CITY · Query terms: US state capital, largest, population · Focus: state capital · Relation: has-population(capital, max).

A naive QA system would just embed the question. Structuring it adds precision. Answer types come from a taxonomy (e.g. LOCATION → country / city / state; also ANIMAL, COLOR, CREATIVE…). Answer-type detection is itself a classification task (hand-written rules, ML or deep learning), and all of these steps are optional.

Step 2: Document and passage retrieval

  1. Keyword selection and IR search over an index of documents.
  2. Segment long documents into shorter passages (paragraphs or chunks).
  3. Rank the passages. Useful features: number of named entities of the right type, number of question keywords present, matching n-grams, keyword proximity, or the cosine similarity between query and passage embeddings (attention-style scores are similarity too).

Step 3: Answer extraction

Example: using a named-entity tagger

Q: "Who is the prime minister of India?" → answer type PERSON.
Passage: "Manmohan Singh, Prime Minister of India, had told left leaders that the deal would not be renegotiated."
Run NER: Manmohan Singh = PERSON. That's the candidate. (Note that the passage is from an old article, which is why time matters; see below.)

Q: "How tall is Mount Everest?" → answer type LENGTH → "The official height of Mount Everest is 29,035 feet."

When several candidates fit ("Who was Queen Victoria's second son?" with many PERSON names in the passage), rank them with rules, ML or deep learning.

Evaluating QA

  • Accuracy: does the answer exactly match the gold answer?
  • Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR): the system returns a ranked list. Score each question by 1/(rank of the first correct answer), then average.
MRR = (1/|Q|) · Σq 1 / rankqscore 0 for a question if no correct answer appears
Worked example

Three questions; the first correct answer appears at ranks 1, 3 and 2.

MRR = (1/1 + 1/3 + 1/2) / 3 = 1.833 / 3 ≈ 0.61

Making answers smarter

  • Relation extraction: store facts as triples, e.g. born-in(Emma Goldman, June 27 1869), prime-minister-of(Modi, India). More structure means faster, better retrieval.
  • Temporal reasoning: "Who was PM in 2005?" vs "now". If someone took a job in 1594, Thoreau (born 1817) can't be the answer, but Cervantes can. LLMs are still weak at time, partly because dates appear in so many formats (26 June, 26/06, '95…). Add timestamps to your data, or use dynamic data pipelines.
  • Geospatial knowledge: containment, direction, borders (GeoNames.org is a common source).

Dense retrievers (the deep-learning way)

Encode the query and every passage as embeddings, score them with a similarity function (cosine, dot product, even Manhattan distance), and rank. These "dense" retrievers replace keyword matching, and can drop explicit answer-type detection if your data doesn't support it.

Training them: contrastive learning

Like negative sampling: pull the query towards positive passages (ones containing the answer) and push it away from negative ones. Hard negatives are passages that look relevant but don't contain the answer. (In class, a passage about fairy tales for a prime-minister question was called a "hard" negative, though the standard term "hard negative" usually means a confusingly similar passage.)

Connection to today

RAG (retrieval-augmented generation) systems and AI agents are essentially advanced QA pipelines: index documents → retrieve the relevant ones → an LLM generates the answer from them.

Building a QA system for a course (like FoVL)
  1. Documents: collect FoVL books, slides and notes.
  2. Questions: write likely student doubts, with gold answers and answer types.
  3. Retrieve the right document/passage, then extract the answer.
  4. Evaluate with accuracy and MRR.
Exam tip

The instructor said the three steps (question processing, document/passage retrieval, answer extraction) and what happens in each are important. Also know factoid vs complex questions, IR vs knowledge-based approaches, and how to compute MRR.

From the lecture slides

  • History: one of the oldest NLP tasks. Punched-card systems in 1961, and Simmons, Klein & McConlogue (1964) used dependency logic to answer English questions. The TREC QA track (Text Retrieval Conference, from 1992) standardised evaluation. IBM Watson won Jeopardy! on 16 Feb 2011 (e.g. "William Wilkinson's 'An Account of the Principalities of Wallachia and Moldavia' inspired this author's novel" → Bram Stoker). Wolfram Alpha is a computational knowledge engine: structured data and reasoning rather than free text.
  • Factoid examples with answer types (slide): Where is the Louvre? → In Paris, France (LOCATION) · Abbreviation for limited partnership? → L.P. (ABBREVIATION) · Names of Odin's ravens? → Huginn and Muninn (ENTITY: animal, list) · Currency in China? → the yuan · Nuts in marzipan? → almonds · Max Roach's instrument? → drums · Stanford's phone number? → 650-723-2300 (NUMERIC). Complex: "What do scholars think about Jefferson's position on dealing with pirates?"
  • Li & Roth (2002) answer-type taxonomy: coarse classes (ENTITY, HUMAN, LOCATION, NUMERIC, ABBREVIATION, DESCRIPTION) with fine-grained types (LOCATION → city, country, mountain…).
  • Rule-based answer typing: regexes like "Who {is|was|are|were} PERSON" (e.g. "Who was the first president of India?" → PERSON) and "PERSON (YEAR – YEAR)" (e.g. "Jawaharlal Nehru (1889–1964)"). Headword rule: the head of the first noun phrase after the wh-word, e.g. "Which city in China has the largest number of foreign financial companies?" → LOCATION, "What is the state flower of California?" → ENTITY (plant).
  • ML answer typing: define a taxonomy → annotate questions (e.g. TREC) → train LR/SVM/neural classifiers on lexical (words, n-grams, wh-cue), syntactic (POS, headwords), semantic (NEs, WordNet) and hand-written (regex) features.
  • Keyword selection (Moldovan et al. 1999), priority order: 1 non-stop words in quotations · 2 NNP words in named entities · 3 complex nominals with adjectival modifiers · 4 other complex nominals · 5 nouns with adjectival modifiers · 6 other nouns · 7 verbs · 8 adverbs · 9 the question focus word · 10 everything else.
  • Candidate-answer ranking features: answer-type match, pattern match, number of question keywords, keyword distance, novelty (a word not in the query), apposition, punctuation location, longest sequence of question terms.
  • Relation extraction in questions: "Whose granddaughter starred in E.T.?" → (acted-in ?x "E.T.") (granddaughter-of ?x ?y). Facts come from Wikipedia infoboxes, DBpedia, Freebase, e.g. author-of("Cao Xue Qin", "Dream of the Red Chamber").
  • Temporal reasoning (Watson): "In 1594 he took a job as a tax collector in Andalusia". Thoreau is a bad answer (born 1817), Cervantes is possible (alive in 1594). Geospatial: Beijing is a good answer for "Asian city"; California is "southwest of Montana" (geonames.org).
  • Dense retriever = bi-encoder: one LSTM encodes the question, another the passage, and the last hidden states are compared by dot product. Train with positive/negative passages and a contrastive loss, choosing positives and negatives by relevance-guided supervision.

Numericals

The slides gave the keyword-priority algorithm and the MRR scoring rule. Both are worked below, followed by practice.

From class From the slides

C1 (slide). Keyword selection for "Who coined the term 'cyberspace' in his novel 'Neuromancer'?"

cyberspace/1 (in quotes), Neuromancer/1 (in quotes), term/4, novel/4 (complex nominals), coined/7 (verb). The query uses the highest-priority words first.

C2 (slide). MRR scoring rule

First answer correct → 1; second → ½; third → ⅓ …; none of the M answers correct → 0. Then take the mean over all N queries.

Practice

P1. Four questions; the first correct answer is at ranks 1, 2, 4 and "not in list". MRR?

(1 + 0.5 + 0.25 + 0)/4 = 0.4375.

P2. A system answers 80 of 100 factoid questions exactly right. Accuracy? If 15 of the wrong ones had the correct answer at rank 2 and 5 never had it, what is the MRR?

Accuracy = 0.80. MRR = (80×1 + 15×0.5 + 5×0)/100 = 0.875.

P3. Query vector q = (1, 0), passages p₁ = (0.8, 0.6), p₂ = (0, 1). Rank them by cosine similarity (dense retrieval).

cos(q, p₁) = 0.8, cos(q, p₂) = 0. Ranking: p₁ then p₂.

P4. Assign priorities: "Who wrote the novel 'Godan' in Hindi literature?"

Godan/1 (in quotes); Hindi literature: if "Hindi" is treated as part of a named entity, 2, otherwise novel/Hindi literature as complex nominals = 4; wrote/7.

P5. Answer type by headword: "Which river flows through Varanasi?" and "What year did India become independent?"

Headword river → LOCATION (river). Headword year → NUMERIC (date).
