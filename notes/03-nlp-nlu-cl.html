03. NLP vs NLU vs Computational Linguistics

ELIZA vs SHRDLU, the recipe for building an NLU system, ambiguity, and the 'NLP trinity'.

Part A · Classical NLP🎙 Language Lecture 3

In 30 seconds

  • NLP = engineering and applications. CL = science: using computation to study language. NLU = both together: represent and understand language.
  • ELIZA (1966) only pattern-matched. Looking right ≠ understanding. SHRDLU represented a small world, reasoned about it, and acted in it.
  • The recipe for an NLU system: corpus (+ annotation) → model → evaluation against humans, repeated in a loop.
  • Main enemy: ambiguity, both lexical (word level) and structural (phrase level).
  • Shannon's idea: measure how surprising a word is, not what it means. This is the root of today's language models.
  • NLP trinity: languages × tasks × algorithms. One algorithm rarely wins everywhere.

Back in the 1970s, AI, NLP, CL and cognitive science weren't separate fields. Everyone shared one goal: build a machine that understands language. Today they're separate departments that collaborate again, much like this course's students who come from many backgrounds.

Two famous historical systems

ELIZA (1966): the polite parrot

Built by Joseph Weizenbaum at MIT to act like a psychotherapist.

User:  Men are all alike.
ELIZA: IN WHAT WAY?
User:  They're always bugging us about something or other.
ELIZA: CAN YOU THINK OF A SPECIFIC EXAMPLE?
User:  Well, my boyfriend made me come here.
ELIZA: YOUR BOYFRIEND MADE YOU COME HERE?
User:  He says I'm depressed much of the time.
ELIZA: I AM SORRY TO HEAR YOU ARE DEPRESSED.

What's going on? ELIZA picks a key word from your sentence (depressed, boyfriend), drops it into a template, and asks a question back. It uses some grammar rules to keep the sentence well formed, but there's no real analysis. It's shallow processing: pattern matching and lexical repetition.

Takeaway

Producing appropriate-looking responses is not the same as understanding.

SHRDLU (Terry Winograd, MIT): understanding inside a tiny world

SHRDLU lived in a "micro-world" of coloured blocks on a table, with a robotic arm. Tell it "Pick up a big red block" and it actually picks up the right block, then confirms "OK."

ELIZA: language→pattern match→respond
SHRDLU: language→represent the world→reason→act (arm)→respond

SHRDLU had declarative knowledge (facts: "this block is red") and procedural knowledge (skills: how to grasp and with what force). It had domain knowledge and grammar built in. It goes beyond appropriate-looking responses and simulates some comprehension, a step towards the Total Turing test.

Limitations: it only worked in its micro-world, only in English, and only with text.

Does it need to reply at all? Strictly, no. Picking up the right block is the proof. But for a language system that interacts with people, the reply acknowledges that the instruction was understood.

The recipe for building an NLU / CL system

  1. Get a corpus (a dataset representing the language). It can be multimodal: text, audio, video.
  2. Annotate it for your task, e.g. tag every word in "John sat near the bank" with its part of speech, or note root words (running → run).
  3. Build a model: machine learning, a distributional model that picks up frequencies, or hand-written grammar rules.
  4. Evaluate against humans. The model's choices should match the choices humans make.
  5. Loop: find where it fails, improve, test again.
Linguistic data→Representation→Model→Empirical evaluation↺

Evaluation metrics

  • Engineering metrics: accuracy, confusion matrix, precision/recall/F1 (for classifiers), BLEU and ROUGE (for generated text).
  • Cognitive metrics (from psycholinguistics):
    • Surprisal: human sentences tend to be probable, so they have low surprisal. A model's output should too. (Surprisal is the "opposite" of probability, covered in topic 05.)
    • Dependency length (Dependency Locality Theory): in real sentences, related words sit closer together than they would in a random shuffle of the same words. Lower dependency length is more human-like.
Example: a typical research setup

Hypothesis: "Human sentences have lower surprisal and shorter dependencies than random word orders."
Corpus: a treebank. Model: compute surprisal and dependency length for real sentences and for shuffled versions. Validate: are real sentences lower on both? Then check whether machine-generated sentences behave the same way.

The classic challenge: ambiguity

Even humans get confused, so imagine the poor machine.

Example: "At last, a computer that understands you like your mother."

Three readings:

  1. The computer understands you as well as your mother does.
  2. The computer understands that you like your mother.
  3. The computer understands you as well as it understands your mother. 😄

Speech makes it worse: an ASR system might hear "…understands your lie cured mother" (the slide's example; in class, "you're like your mother"). And "mother" has a technical meaning in vinegar-making (the culture that turns wine into vinegar), so domain knowledge matters too.

Example: "Copy the local patient files to disk."

Is local describing the patient (a patient from nearby) or the files (files stored locally)? The class found that even modern chatbots trip on puzzles like this.

Lexical ambiguity

One word has several meanings.
"John sat near the bank." (river or money?)

Structural ambiguity

One sentence can be grouped in different ways.
"John saw Mary with a telescope." Does "with a telescope" attach to John or to Mary?

Can we fix it with more data? Two powerful ideas

1. "You shall know a word by the company it keeps" (J.R. Firth)

Words that appear together (co-occur) tell us about meaning. Fisherman near bank means river bank. With enough data, the co-occurrence counts sort out many ambiguities. This leads to the question: Is NLP just a massive knowledge-engineering (data) project?

2. Claude Shannon: measure surprise, not meaning

Shannon, the father of information theory, said: "I don't care what a message means. I care how surprising it is given what came before."

Example: "Apples and ___"

Most people say oranges. P(oranges | apples and) is high, so it carries little information. If someone says "apples and carburettors", that's very surprising and carries a lot of information.

high probability → low information   |   low probability → high information

This one idea runs today's large language models: they predict the next word from conditional probabilities.

"Computational linguistics without linguistics?"

Modern LLMs are driven mostly by frequency and next-word prediction. So where did the L in CL go? The professor argued for bringing linguistics back:

  • Low-resource languages (e.g. Munda in Jharkhand) have very little digital text, so data-hungry AI does poorly. Even Hindi has lots of text but little linguistically annotated text.
  • Negation: studies showed BERT-style models struggle with "not", double negatives, and language-specific negation patterns.
  • Coreference resolution: "Ram is a good boy. He went to school." Linking he → Ram is still hard for powerful models.
  • Rare constructions: humans handle rare but meaningful patterns easily. Models barely see them in training, so they don't learn them.

The NLP trinity

That's why the field is so wide: there are many problems, in many languages, with many algorithms. It's also why big labs now chase universal, multilingual models.

Exam tip

Know the NLP / CL / NLU differences, what ELIZA vs SHRDLU show (pattern matching vs representation, reasoning and action), lexical vs structural ambiguity with examples, and the "high probability = low information" idea.

Try it

Write one sentence with lexical ambiguity and one with structural ambiguity. For each, what extra context would remove the ambiguity?

Closing discussion questions (from the slides)
  1. If a machine produces a completely appropriate response to a sentence, does that mean it understands it? (Think ELIZA: no.)
  2. What's the difference between processing language and understanding it? (Processing maps input to output; understanding builds meaning grounded in the world, like SHRDLU.)
  3. If a model learns statistical patterns from enormous amounts of text, what exactly has it learned? (Distributional regularities of form, which may or may not amount to meaning.)

Recommended reading: Lillian Lee, "I'm sorry Dave, I'm afraid I can't do that" (2001); Mark Steedman, "Romantics and Revolutionaries".

Practice (application questions)

No numericals were taught in this lecture. These questions check the concepts.

P1. Classify the ambiguity: (a) "The chicken is ready to eat." (b) "She went to the bat."

(a) Structural: is the chicken eating, or being eaten? The words are the same but the roles differ. (b) Lexical: bat could be the animal or the cricket bat.

P2. Is a grammar checker that flags "I eats" an NLP, CL or NLU system?

Mainly NLP (an application). If it were built to test a theory of agreement in human grammar, it would be CL.

P3. Which word carries more information: P(oranges | apples and) = 0.6 or P(carburettors | apples and) = 0.0001?

carburettors. Lower probability means more surprise, which means more information (−log₂ 0.0001 ≈ 13.3 bits vs −log₂ 0.6 ≈ 0.74 bits).
