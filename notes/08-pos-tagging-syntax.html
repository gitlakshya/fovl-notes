08. POS Tagging, HMMs & Syntax

Tagging words with HMMs, chunking, and the two ways to draw a sentence's structure.

Part A · Classical NLP🎙 Language Lecture 9

In 30 seconds

  • Basic pipeline: sentence segmentation → tokenization → stemming / lemmatization → POS tagging.
  • POS tagging gives every word a grammatical category. Hard because one word can have many tags (book a ticket vs read a book).
  • Taggers can be rule/lookup-based, statistical (generative like HMM, or discriminative) or neural.
  • An HMM sees the words but not the tags. It picks the tag sequence that maximises transition × emission probabilities, efficiently with Viterbi.
  • Chunking groups words into phrases with B-I-O tags (also used for named entities).
  • Two ways to draw sentence structure: constituency (nested phrases) and dependency (head → dependent arrows).

Exam tip: stem vs lemma

"What is the difference between a stem and a lemma?" A stem is what's left after chopping affixes and may not be a valid word (leav). A lemma is the dictionary root word and is always a valid word (leaf, child). Give examples.

The in-class activity used NLTK on a toy document and the Brown corpus. The professor asked students to write the code themselves without AI help, because these are fundamentals.

Part-of-speech tagging

"The student reads books" → The/DET student/NOUN reads/VERB books/NOUN.

How many tags? The classic school list has 8 (noun, pronoun, verb, adjective, adverb, preposition, conjunction, interjection). Real tagsets are bigger:

  • Universal tagset: about 12 (adds numbers, punctuation, etc.)
  • Penn Treebank: 45 tags
  • Brown corpus: about 87 tags (proper vs common nouns, many verb forms…)

The finer the distinctions, the more tags you need.

Why is tagging hard? The same word, different jobs

Examples
  • "I read a book" → NOUN  |  "Please book a ticket" → VERB
  • "She can dance" → modal verb  |  "a can of soda" → NOUN
  • "The fisherman sat near the bank" → the tag (and meaning) of bank depends on context

You use two clues: the word's own identity (book is usually a noun) and its context (after "please", it's acting as a verb).

Headline puzzle: "British Left Waffles on Falkland Islands"

Reading 1: British/N Left/V Waffles/N on/P Falkland/N Islands/N, i.e. the British left behind some waffles.
Reading 2: British/N Left/N Waffles/V on/P Falkland/N Islands/N, i.e. the British political Left is waffling (being indecisive) about the islands.

Two valid tag sequences. How do you choose? Probability: pick the more likely sequence.

Tagging as machine learning

Training data is (word, tag) pairs labelled by linguists (the gold standard). Given a new sentence, predict the hidden tag of each word. It's a multi-class classification problem (one of 12, 45 or 87 classes), done for a whole sequence, so it's called sequence tagging.

Three families of taggers

FamilyIdeaExamples
Rule-based / lookupTables or hand rulesdefault tagger, regex tagger, n-gram (lookup-table) tagger
Statistical: generativeModel the joint P(words, tags) = P(tags) · P(words | tags)HMM tagger
Statistical: discriminativeModel P(tag | word, context) directlyPerceptron tagger (uses prefixes/suffixes and words on both sides)
NeuralWord and tag embeddingsBiLSTM / transformer taggers (most accurate)
Analogy: generative vs discriminative

A generative model is like an author who knows how stories are written: "nouns tend to produce words like student, dog, book, and a determiner is usually followed by a noun." A discriminative model is like an examiner who only learns to answer: "given this word in this context, which tag?"

Refresher: finite-state automata (FSA)

An FSA is a 5-tuple: states Q, alphabet Σ, start state q₀, final states F, transition function δ.

Example: the "sheep language" baa…a!

q₀ —b→ q₁ —a→ q₂ —a→ q₃ (q₃ loops on a) —!→ q₄ (final, drawn as a double circle).
Accepts: baa!, baaaa! (regex baa+!). Rejects: bba! (no transition for a second b), bac!.

A simpler FSA can accept just "cat" and "car": q₀ —c→ q₁ —a→ q₂ —t or r→ q₃.

An HMM is an FSA that emits symbols with probabilities as it moves between states.

Hidden Markov Model (HMM) tagger

Words are observed; tags are hidden. The HMM is a 5-tuple:

SymbolNameIn tagging
Qstatesthe tags (N, V, ADJ…)
Atransition probabilitiesP(tagi | tagi−1), e.g. P(N | ADJ)
Oobservationsthe vocabulary (words)
Bemission probabilities (observation likelihoods)P(word | tag), e.g. P(dog | N)
πstart probabilitiesP(first tag)
P(words, tags) = π(t₁)·P(w₁|t₁) · Πi≥2 P(ti|ti−1)·P(wi|ti)   →  best tags = argmax over tag sequencestransition × emission at every step

Rules: outgoing transition probabilities from each state sum to 1, and emission probabilities from each state sum to 1.

Worked example: tag "fish swim"

Tags: N (noun), V (verb). Learned tables:

Best: fish/N swim/V. If two sequences tie, the tagger picks one (you could change the code to return both).

Viterbi: avoiding the explosion

With Q tags and T words there are QT sequences. With 87 tags and a 10-word sentence that's 87¹⁰ (about 2.5 × 10¹⁹). Brute force is hopeless.

Viterbi in one line

Dynamic programming: at each word, keep only the best path into each tag, and extend those best paths to the next word instead of starting over. Cost drops to about T × Q² operations.

Analogy

Driving Jodhpur → Jaipur → Delhi: once you know the best route to Jaipur, you don't re-plan the Jodhpur–Jaipur part when you continue to Delhi. You extend the best route you already have.

Chunking and B-I-O tags

A chunk is a group of words that belong together (fast food, heavy rain, the yellow dog). IOB encoding turns chunking into tagging:

WordWesawtheyellowdog
POSPRPVBDDTJJNN
ChunkB-NPB-VPB-NPI-NPI-NP

B = beginning of a chunk, I = inside, O = outside. The same trick is used for named-entity recognition (B-PER, I-PER, B-LOC…; see topic 14) and helps with parsing.

Sentence structure: two formalisms

1. Constituency (phrase structure)

Words group into phrases (constituents), and phrases nest inside bigger phrases.

Test for a constituent: it moves as a unit.
✔ John talked [to the children] [about drugs] → John talked [about drugs] [to the children].
✘ *John talked drugs to the children about. (Splitting the phrase breaks the sentence.)

Grammar rules: an NP contains a noun, a VP a verb, a PP a preposition, and so on. Trees can also be written in bracket ("Lisp") notation as above.

2. Dependency

No phrase nodes. Words link directly to each other through head → dependent arrows, which can be labelled with grammatical relations.

Properties: nodes = words, arcs = dependency relations (labelled or not), arcs go one way, and there are no cycles (a directed acyclic graph, in fact a tree). Labels such as subject and object are argument dependencies; others are modifiers (determiner, noun modifier…). "A boy paints the wall": boy = subject of paints, wall = object.

Constituency

Phrases are non-terminal nodes (NP, VP, S); words are leaves. Built on context-free grammar.

Dependency

Only words; explicit head–dependent relations; the arc labels give grammatical functions.

Attachment ambiguity

"One morning I shot an elephant in my pajamas."

(Groucho Marx's joke: "How he got into my pajamas I'll never know.")
Parse 1: [in my pajamas] attaches to the verb phrase, so I was wearing the pajamas.
Parse 2: [an elephant in my pajamas] is one NP, so the elephant was wearing them.
And "shot" itself is ambiguous: a gun or a camera?

How to resolve it? Probability again: give probabilities to grammar rules, score each tree, and choose the more probable one.

Tools

NLTK (great for learning; has a chapter on analysing sentence structure), spaCy (fast, production-ready: tokenizer, NER, dependency parser), Stanza (Stanford NLP: POS, lemmas, dependency parsing in many languages).

Exam tip

Given start, transition and emission tables, compute the most likely tag sequence for a short sentence (enumerate all QT sequences and take the max). Also: stem vs lemma, generative vs discriminative, the HMM 5-tuple, IOB tagging, constituency vs dependency, and why Viterbi is needed.

Try it

Using the tables above, could "swim fish" ever come out as V N? Compute all four sequences for "swim fish" and check. (Answer: yes. V N = 0.4 × 0.5 × 0.8 × 0.6 = 0.096, the highest of the four.)

From the POS-Tagging lab (InClassActivity3, NLTK notebook)

The lab walks through the pipeline in code: sentence segmentation (Punkt), word tokenisation ("Don't" → "Do", "n't"), stemming vs lemmatisation ("sportingly" → "sportingli" with Porter vs "sport" with Lancaster), then tagging.

The first tagger is the default tagger: it gives every word the same tag (NN). It sounds silly, but it is the baseline every real tagger must beat. Its accuracy is simply the share of tokens that really are nouns (roughly 13% in the Brown corpus). The exercises then use the perceptron tagger on tricky sentences like "Colorless green ideas sleep furiously" and count tag frequencies in Brown and the Penn Treebank.

From the lecture slides

  • Types of POS tagger (slide): rule-based/look-up (regex tagger, default and n-gram taggers) · statistical (generative: HMM; discriminative: Maximum Entropy tagger) · neural (BiLSTM taggers; transformer models like BERT, spaCy pipelines, Stanza).
  • Generative: models P(W, T), "how likely are these words and tags together?", then picks argmaxT P(W, T). Discriminative: models P(T | W), e.g. it learns that reads is a verb in "The student reads books".
  • FSA formal example: A = <Q, Σ, q₀, δ, F> with Q = {q₀, q₁, q₂, q₃}, Σ = {c, a, t, r}, F = {q₃}, δ = {<q₀,c,q₁>, <q₁,a,q₂>, <q₂,t,q₃>, <q₂,r,q₃>}. It accepts "cat" and "car".
  • Sheep language: "baaa" reaches the accept state at the end of the string, so accepted. "bb" has no accepting state (it stops at q₁, which isn't final), so rejected. "bac": transition not defined, so rejected.
  • Two kinds of finite-state machine: output on each transition edge (Mealy) vs output on each state (Moore). An HMM is a probabilistic Moore machine: start in state i with probability πᵢ, move i → j with P(j | i), emit word o from state i with P(o | i).
  • N-grams vs HMMs for "British Left Waffles on Falkland Islands": the bigram model multiplies P(British)·P(Left|British)·…; the HMM multiplies P(N)·P(British|N)·P(N|N)·P(Left|N)·P(V|N)·P(Waffles|V)·P(P|V)·P(on|P)·P(N|P)·P(Falkland|N)·P(N|N)·P(Islands|N).
  • Chunking with IOB (exact slide example): We/PRP B-NP · saw/VBD B-VP · the/DT B-NP · yellow/JJ I-NP · dog/NN I-NP · ./PUNC O → [NP We] [VP saw [NP the yellow dog]].
  • NER with IOB: United/B-ORG cancelled/O the/O flight/O from/O San/B-LOC Francisco/I-LOC.
  • Chunkers range from regex chunkers to n-gram chunkers (tag patterns) to classifiers using both tags and words. NER also uses classifiers or neural sequence taggers.
  • Perceptron tagger: error-driven learning that scores the whole sentence's tag sequence (not word by word), so it can capture distant phenomena. RNN taggers are also very effective.
  • Dependency labels: argument (subj, obj, iobj) and modifier (det, nmod, vmod). Treebanks: Universal Dependencies (dependency) and the Penn Treebank (constituency).

C1. Validity check

Outgoing arcs: A: 1/3 + 2/3 = 1 ✔; N: 1/10 + 9/10 = 1 ✔. Emissions: A: 1 ✔; N: 3 × 1/3 = 1 ✔. So these are valid transition and emission probabilities.

C3. "British Left" (slide)

S1 = NN: P(N)P(British|N)P(N|N)P(Left|N). S2 = NV: P(N)P(British|N)P(V|N)P(Left|V). With the learned tables, P(S2) > P(S1), so "Left" is tagged as a verb.

C4. How many sequences?

Length 2 with 2 states: 2² = 4. With q states and length T: qT. For a 4-word phrase with words from {killer, crazy, clown, problem}: 2⁴ = 16 sequences. Brute force is exponential, so we use Viterbi (dynamic programming).

P5. Which strings does the cat/car FSA accept: "cat", "car", "ca", "cart"?

Accepts cat and car (ending in q₃). "ca" ends in q₂ (not final), so rejected. "cart" has no transition from q₃, so rejected.

P6. Penn Treebank (45 tags), 6-word sentence: brute-force sequences and rough Viterbi cost?

45⁶ ≈ 8.3 × 10⁹ sequences. Viterbi ≈ 6 × 45² = 12,150 steps.
