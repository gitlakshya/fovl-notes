18. Sentiment & Aspect-Based Analysis

Opinions, attitudes, sarcasm, lexicons, and aspect-based sentiment with LSTMs and attention.

Part B · Neural NLP🎙 Neural NLP Lecture 7 (15 Nov, part 2)

In 30 seconds

  • We rely on others' opinions (which car, which school, which professor), but reviews are many, biased and sometimes fake.
  • Sentiment analysis sits under subjectivity analysis: the study of private states (opinions, emotions, beliefs) that can't be objectively verified.
  • An attitude has a holder, a target, a type (positive/negative or fine-grained emotions) and the text that carries it.
  • Hard parts: negation, sarcasm, subtle wording, sentiment that flips mid-review, and multiple aspects.
  • Aspect-based sentiment analysis (ABSA): "battery is awesome but camera is poor" → battery = positive, camera = negative. Solved with LSTMs (TD-LSTM, TC-LSTM), attention, and triplet/quad extraction.

Why opinions matter (and why they're messy)

Before deciding, we gather opinions: Which car should I buy? Which school should I apply to? Whom should I vote for? But once you have hundreds of reviews, how do you actually decide?

Example: spotting fake reviews

A set of glowing reviews for a moving company on Yellow Pages had red flags:

  • All posted on the same date.
  • Each reviewer had reviewed only one business.
  • All rated 5 stars.
  • Reviewers knew the exact names of employees, which suggests insiders.
  • Too many positive mentions.

Deciding what to trust is part of opinion mining.

Business uses ("business intelligence"): What do consumers think of the iPhone? What do they dislike? What features should we add? Studies even linked Twitter mood (calm vs anxious) to stock-market movements about 3 days later: emotions shape decisions, but with a delay.

Subjectivity analysis

Subjectivity is the linguistic expression of someone's opinions, sentiments, emotions and beliefs, i.e. their private states, which aren't open to objective verification. It's an umbrella over closely related, often interchangeable tasks: sentiment analysis, opinion mining, review mining, appraisal extraction. Sources include blogs, editorials, reviews and news.

Anatomy of an attitude

Sentiment analysis = detecting attitudes: "enduring, affectively coloured beliefs, dispositions towards objects or persons" (longlasting, emotion-influenced tendencies to like or dislike).

ComponentQuestionExample
Holder (source)Who holds the opinion?"Customers hate the service"
Target (aspect)What is it about?"I love this phone", "The battery is bad"
TypeWhat feeling?Fine-grained: love, hate, like, desire, value… or coarse: positive / negative / neutral
TextWhich span carries it?a sentence, a whole review, a tweet

Difficulty levels: simplest = is this text positive or negative? Harder = rate it 1–5. Advanced = detect the target, the source and complex attitude types.

Data and its challenges

An early benchmark: the polarity dataset of IMDb movie reviews labelled positive or negative ("Snake Eyes is the most aggravating kind of movie: the kind that shows so much potential then becomes unbelievably disappointing" → negative).

ChallengeExample / note
Messy textHTML/XML tags, Twitter handles, hashtags, phone numbers, emoticons
Negation"I didn't like this movie" vs "I really like this movie". A classic trick: prefix every word after a negation with NOT_ (didn't NOT_like NOT_this NOT_movie) up to the next punctuation.
Subtle / no negative wordsPerfume review: "If you are reading this because it is your darling fragrance, please wear it at home exclusively, and tape the windows shut." Negative, with no negative word.
Sarcasmstill an open research problem
Thwarted expectations / alternating sentimentpositive… then negative… then positive. The ending often decides.
Aspect / subject changes"The food is very cheap" (positive) vs "The service is very cheap" (negative)
A student's question: "I barely like this movie"

There's no explicit "not", but the meaning is weakly positive or close to neutral. Cases like this (intensity, hedges) need special data and models. They're exceptions you have to design for explicitly.

Approach 1: sentiment lexicons

Lists of words with known polarity: LIWC, Bing Liu's opinion lexicon, SentiWordNet, and lexicons for other languages (Hindi) and domains (movies).

✔ Pros

Fast, need no training data, decent initial accuracy.

✘ Cons

Words have multiple senses, multi-word phrases, domain-specific meanings ("cheap", "unpredictable": good in a thriller, bad in a car).

Approach 2: neural classifiers

Sentence-level (coarse-grained): run the sentence through an RNN/LSTM, take the last hidden state, pass it through a linear layer and softmax over {positive, negative, neutral}.

Thezoomanddisplayareawesome→ RNN → hT → linear → softmax →positive 0.93
Where do labels come from?

The softmax gives one probability per class (e.g. positive 0.5, negative 0.3, neutral 0.2). The prediction is the highest one. You don't assign the lower numbers to classes by hand; the model learns all three from labelled data.

Aspect-based sentiment analysis (ABSA)

"Its battery is awesome but the camera is very poor."

Overall polarity is mixed and meaningless. ABSA splits it: battery → positive, camera → negative. Similarly, "the food was good but the service was bad."

  1. Aspect term extraction: find the aspects. "I like the service and the staff, but not the food" → service, staff, food. "Awesome form factor and great battery life" → form factor, battery life.
  2. Aspect polarity: give each aspect a sentiment. Some aspects are implicit: "It was very expensive for what you get" (aspect = price, never named).

Coarse-grained = sentence level. Fine-grained = aspect level. The field spans languages (English, Hindi, code-mixed and transliterated text), domains (e-commerce…) and techniques (feature-based → deep learning).

LSTM models for ABSA (Tang et al., 2016)

ModelIdea
LSTMTreats the target word like any other word, so it ignores which aspect we're asking about.
TD-LSTM (target-dependent)Two LSTMs: one reads from the start up to the target, the other reads from the end back to the target. Combine both final states, so the target is the meeting point.
TC-LSTM (target-connection)Like TD-LSTM, but the target word's vector is concatenated to every input word, so each word is read in relation to the aspect.

Accuracy improved from LSTM → TD-LSTM → TC-LSTM.

Attention for ABSA

Design the attention so it focuses on words related to the aspect. Visualising the weights shows whether the model looked at "awesome" for battery and "poor" for camera. Benchmarks come from the SemEval shared tasks.

Beyond pairs: triplets and quads

TaskOutput for "The pizza is delicious"
Aspect sentiment triplet extraction(aspect = pizza, opinion = delicious, polarity = positive)
Aspect sentiment quad prediction(pizza, delicious, positive, category = food quality)

Multilinguality: out-of-vocabulary words and English-only resources led to bilingual word embeddings and translation-based approaches.

  • More challenges: similar words, different sentiment ("This movie is not good" vs "No movie can be better than this"); different wording, same sentiment ("extremely useless phone" / "wasted my money" / "could have bought an iPhone instead"); product-name variants (G-phone, Google-phone); "waste of time" (−) vs "throw your waste here" (neutral); sarcasm ("All the features you want, too bad they don't work :-P"); Dorothy Parker on Katherine Hepburn: "She runs the gamut of emotions from A to B"; user-written grammar and spelling; subject centrality; thwarted expectations ("This film should be brilliant… However, it can't hold up"); almost no resources for Indian languages.
  • Tokenisation issues: HTML/XML markup, Twitter names and hashtags, capitalisation (keep ALL-CAPS words), phone numbers and dates, emoticons. Which words to use? Only adjectives, or all words? All words worked better on the Pang & Lee data.
  • Public opinion: Twitter sentiment tracks the Gallup consumer-confidence poll (O'Connor et al. 2010). CALM mood (not "happy") predicted the Dow Jones about 3 days later (Bollen et al. 2011), because markets depend on confidence and lack of panic.
  • Coarse vs fine grained (slide example): five camera-review sentences give a single sentence-level rating of 3.5★, but aspect level shows Price 4.5★, Zoom 5.0★, Picture quality 4.5★, Battery 1.5★, Display 1.0★. Much more informative for a buyer.
  • ABSA examples: "The speed, the design… lightyears ahead" → Speed +, Design + · "Tech support would not fix the problem unless I bought your plan for $150 plus" → Tech support − · "Certainly not the best sushi in New York, however, it is always fresh, and the place is very clean" → Sushi conflict, Place +.
  • Pipelines: traditional = feature engineering (n-grams, cue words, lexicons) + SVM / decision tree. Deep = embeddings + CNN/LSTM + MLP → Positive / Negative / Neutral / Conflict.
  • CNN for sentiment: a linear layer slides over consecutive word pairs ("The movie", "movie is", "is very", "very good"), then max-pooling over each feature turns a variable-length input into a fixed vector (dominated by "very good"), then a feed-forward layer outputs the label. Word order changes ("very good was the movie") still give the same max-pooled features.
  • TC-LSTM detail: each word is paired with the target vector: LSTML(Its, battery) → (battery, battery); LSTMR: (poor, battery) → (is, battery) → … → (battery, battery).
  • Akhtar et al. 2018 (cross- and multi-linguality): low-resource Hindi/French ABSA suffers from OOV words. Use bilingual word embeddings (Luong et al. 2015: skip-gram where a word's context includes aligned words from both languages, trained on a parallel corpus). Translate OOV words to English for a second lookup (e.g. two Hindi spellings of "combination"), and extract features with English lexicons (Bing Liu, MPQA, SentiWordNet). Multilingual setup: train and test on Hindi/French. Cross-lingual setup: train on English, test on Hindi/French.
  • Attention for ABSA (Wang et al. 2016): AT-LSTM adds the aspect at the attention layer. ATAE-LSTM adds the aspect embedding at both the input and attention layers. A multi-word aspect is averaged: va = (embbattery + emblife)/2.
  • ASTE: "The pizza is delicious, but the service is terrible" → (pizza, delicious, positive), (service, terrible, negative). Quad: adds the aspect category (food, service).

Practice

P1. A classifier's softmax gives positive 0.5, negative 0.3, neutral 0.2. Prediction?

Positive, the highest probability. You don't assign the other numbers to labels by hand.

P2. Apply the NOT_ trick to: "I didn't like the plot, but the music was great."

I didn't NOT_like NOT_the NOT_plot , but the music was great. (Negation stops at the punctuation.)

P3. ABSA: give (aspect, opinion, polarity) triplets for "Battery life is amazing but the screen scratches easily."

(battery life, amazing, positive), (screen, scratches easily, negative).

P4. What fraction of the MPQA and Bing Liu lexicons is negative?

MPQA: 4,912/6,885 ≈ 71%. Bing Liu: 4,783/6,786 ≈ 70%. Negative words dominate both.

P5. By how many accuracy points does TC-LSTM beat LSTM in Tang et al.? And ATAE-LSTM over LSTM (3-class) in Wang et al.?

0.715 − 0.665 = 5.0 points. 84.0 − 82.0 = 2.0 points.

P6. Aspect embedding for "battery life" if emb(battery) = [0.4, 0.2] and emb(life) = [0.0, 0.6].

va = average = [0.2, 0.4].

P7. With a bigram CNN feature, which bigram wins max-pooling for "the movie is very good" if the feature scores are 0.1, 0.0, 0.3, 0.9?

"very good" (0.9). Its value is passed to the classifier.
