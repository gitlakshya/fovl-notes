What is computer vision?

Look at a photo of a kitchen. In a split second you know it is a kitchen, see the chairs and the table, know which chair is nearer, and could walk around it in your head. A computer just gets a grid of numbers (pixel brightness values). Computer vision is the job of turning that grid of numbers into the understanding you get for free.

Analogy

Pixels are like the letters of a book you can't read yet. Edges are words, surfaces and depth are sentences, and recognising the object is understanding the story. Vision, like language, is built in layers.

The slides list the tasks CV covers: object detection and classification, scene understanding, semantic segmentation, 3-D reconstruction, tracking, human pose estimation, activity recognition and visual question answering (VQA), where vision meets language.

A short history

The slides walk from biological vision (octopus, bee and chameleon eyes) and the camera obscura (Leonardo da Vinci, 16th century) to neuroscience: some brain areas respond more to faces than to houses (Kanwisher et al., 1997). Then come the computational milestones:

Marr: vision happens in stages

David Marr argued that seeing is computation, not talent. A 2-D image is ambiguous: the same object looks different under other lighting, distances or viewpoints, or when partly hidden. So the brain builds intermediate representations first.

  • Pixels tell us what is visible,
  • edges tell us where structures begin and end,
  • depth tells us how those structures are arranged, and
  • the 3-D model tells us what the object is.
Why "2½-D" and not 3-D? (slide Q2)

Because it contains depth, but only for the surfaces you can see from where you stand. You can tell the front of a basketball is curved, but you can't see its back. A full 3-D model is object-centred; the 2½-D sketch is viewer-centred.

Link to the language half: this is the same Marr whose three levels of analysis (computational, algorithmic, implementation) appear in topic 02.

Hand-designed features: SIFT and HOG

SIFT (Lowe, 1999)

SIFT finds keypoints, small distinctive patches such as corners or blobs, and describes each with a vector, so the same point can be matched across photos.

Slide Q3: what makes a feature point "good"?

It is distinctive (unique texture), stable under scale and rotation, and reliable across lighting and viewpoint changes. Corners and blobs work best because they are easy to pin down.

Slide Q4: if I rotate or scale a photo of a building, does SIFT find the same keypoints?

Yes. SIFT searches over a scale space (the image blurred and shrunk at many sizes) and gives each keypoint a dominant orientation. The descriptor is measured relative to that scale and orientation, so it does not change.

HOG (Dalal & Triggs, 2005)

For every pixel, HOG measures the gradient magnitude (how strongly brightness changes) and the gradient orientation (which way it changes). It splits the image into small cells and builds a histogram of edge directions in each cell.

Why it works for people

Clothes and colours change, but the arrangement of edges stays similar: curved edges around the head, diagonal ones at the shoulders, vertical ones along the body, two separate strips for the legs. HOG cares about edge directions, not exact pixel values.

Slide Q5: where does HOG fail?

On objects that are highly deformable (animals in varied poses), lack strong edges (smooth surfaces), or are occluded / in clutter, because HOG relies on a consistent pattern of edge directions on a fixed grid.

3-D reconstruction from photos

Thousands of tourist photos of the Colosseum can be turned into a 3-D point cloud (Agarwal et al., 2009).

Slide Q6: how is 3-D recovered from flat photos?

Find the same feature points (e.g. SIFT) in several photos, estimate where each camera was and which way it pointed, then use triangulation: the 3-D point must lie where the viewing rays from the different cameras meet.

Big data and deep learning

Datasets grew from Caltech-101 and PASCAL VOC to ImageNet: 22,000 categories and 15 million images. The ImageNet challenge (ILSVRC) used 1,000 classes and about 1.43 million images, and scored a model as correct if the true label was among its top-5 guesses. In 2012 a deep convolutional network (AlexNet) cut the error sharply. Every winner since has been a deeper network (VGG, GoogLeNet, ResNet), and accuracy on ImageNet has kept climbing.

The big shift

Before 2012, people designed features (SIFT, HOG) and trained a simple classifier on top. After 2012, the network learns the features from data. The same shift happened in NLP: hand-built features → learned word embeddings (topic 10).

Segmentation

Detection draws a box around each object (the COCO dataset). Segmentation goes further and labels every pixel.

Slide question: semantic vs instance segmentation

Semantic segmentation gives every chair the same colour: it knows what is a chair, but not which chair. Instance segmentation also separates each chair into its own object with its own colour (the LVIS dataset).

CLIP and DALL·E: where vision meets language

CLIP (OpenAI, 2021) trains an image encoder and a text encoder together, so that a photo and its caption end up as nearby vectors. Training uses a contrastive loss: matching image–caption pairs are pulled together and mismatched pairs pushed apart, much like negative sampling in word2vec (topic 11).

To classify an image with no task-specific training ("zero-shot"), CLIP embeds prompts like "a photo of a dog", "a photo of a cat" and picks the one with the highest cosine similarity to the image.

DALL·E goes the other way: it creates images from text ("an armchair in the shape of an avocado"). The slides end on low-level vision, such as phone cameras that brighten night photos (Pixel "Night Sight").

Practice Quick checks

P1. A CLIP model gives these cosine similarities between one image and three prompts: "a dog" 0.31, "a cat" 0.24, "a car" 0.08. What does it predict, and what are the softmax probabilities with temperature 0.01 (i.e. divide scores by 0.01)?

Scaled scores: 31, 24, 8. Softmax: e31 : e24 : e8. Divide by e31: 1 : e−7 ≈ 0.00091 : e−23 ≈ 0.

P(dog) ≈ 1/1.00091 ≈ 0.999, P(cat) ≈ 0.0009, P(car) ≈ 0. Prediction: dog. A small temperature makes the softmax very confident.

P2. An image has 2 people and 3 chairs. How many distinct labels (colours) are in its semantic segmentation map versus its instance segmentation map? (Count the background as one label.)

Semantic: one label per class → person, chair, background = 3.

Instance: one label per object → 2 people + 3 chairs + background = 6.

P3. HOG uses 9 orientation bins per cell. A 64 × 128 pedestrian window is split into 8 × 8-pixel cells. How many cells are there, and how many numbers do the cell histograms hold (before block normalisation)?

Cells: (64/8) × (128/8) = 8 × 16 = 128 cells. Numbers: 128 × 9 = 1,152. (The standard detector then groups 2 × 2 cells into overlapping blocks: 7 × 15 blocks × 36 = 3,780 features.)
