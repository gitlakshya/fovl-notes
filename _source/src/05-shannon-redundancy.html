A wartime story: Turing and Shannon at Bell Labs

1943, World War II. Countries sent coded messages about troop movements and attacks, and each side desperately wanted to intercept and break the other's codes. Two young scientists were working on exactly this, at Bell Labs in New York:

Alan Turing: the computation guy

Proved the Central Limit Theorem on his own as an undergraduate (King's College, Cambridge); PhD under Alonzo Church (Church–Turing thesis). Loved chess, crosswords and typewriters. Helped break the German Enigma code.

Claude Shannon: the information guy

Electrical engineering and maths at Michigan. His MIT master's thesis founded digital circuit design. His PhD was on algebra for theoretical genetics. The father of information theory.

They met over lunch. They never discussed their classified projects, but they talked about the big shared question: how can information and computation be represented formally? Shannon dreamed of an "electronic brain". Turing said he only wanted a mundane brain, one that could take over boring tasks humans don't want to do (automation). They were talking about thinking machines before transistors existed.

The common thread was logic, and mapping symbols to numbers. Turing encoded instructions as numbers, which is the root of how we represent words as vectors and tensors today. Shannon mapped logic onto electrical switches and relays.

What is "the signal"?

Suppose a codebreaker receives evidence about a location. Before, there was uncertainty: it could be one of several places. The new evidence makes some places more likely and others less. What's the signal: the probability itself, or the thing that changed it?

Turing's insight

Both count, but the really useful signal is the information that changes the probability of an event.

Example: class cancelled

You're 100% sure you're going to class (P = 1). Then a message arrives: "Class cancelled due to a technical issue." Your probability drops from 1 to 0. That message was very informative because it changed what you believed. This insight helped Shannon build his mathematical theory of cryptography.

Redundancy: the predictable part of language

Raw data looks like junk until you find patterns in it. Shannon looked for them in the most available data he had: English. Language can be counted (it's discrete), and if it has patterns, it should be measurable.

Redundancy = the part of a message you could leave out and still be understood, because it's predictable.

Analogy: the telegram

In the old days, a telegram cost money per letter. So people wrote "ARRIVING TUESDAY STOP SEND CAR" instead of "I will be arriving on Tuesday, so please send the car." You squeeze out the redundancy to save money without losing meaning. Shannon asked: how much can a message be compressed before information is lost?

Example: q is always followed by u

question, quantity, quick, quack, request, qualify, quantize. After q, the u is almost certain, so it's redundant. Often a vowel after "qu" is fairly predictable too. In probability terms, P(u | q) is very high.

Example: "Keep the flower on the ___"

Everyone fills in table. Because it's predictable, you could stop early and still be understood. This is the fill-in-the-blank game that every language model plays.

Shannon's game: n-th order approximations

Shannon (with his wife, as the story goes) generated text with random-number tables, adding more English statistics at each step. Use 27 symbols: 26 letters + space.

What this shows

As we add statistical constraints, text looks more and more like English, even though it still means nothing. Language can be modelled as the output of a process that generates discrete events with certain probabilities. Notice the open question too: does imitating the frequency patterns ever capture meaning?

Why not keep increasing n forever? (A student's question.) Long sequences (7-grams, 8-grams) are rare, so their counts are unreliable, and computation grows. On the other hand, some long-range dependencies matter. In Hindi, once you hear "Ram ne…", you know the verb will be in the perfective/past form however long the sentence gets. Humans have cognitive limits (memory), social limits (time pressure) and informational limits. Machines have fewer, but this course studies language as humans produce it.

Context reduces uncertainty: the reading-lamp game

Played live in class. Guess the next word at each step:

Context so farPossible next wordsUncertainty
A …book, watch, monkey, car, idea… (almost any noun or adjective)very high
A small …bus, kitten, clip, paper, window…high
A small oblong …table, desk, box, whiteboard…medium
A small oblong reading …lamp, table…low
A small oblong reading lamp on the …desk, tablevery low

More choices = more entropy (randomness). Fewer choices = less entropy. This is the basic intuition behind every language model: the context narrows down what comes next.

Can more context ever increase uncertainty?

Yes. The professor gave a Hindi sentence stuffed with names (Ram, Sita, Gita…) where it became unclear who did what to whom. Several words sharing features (all animate nouns) can confuse you. More words don't always help. Good context does.

Analogy: "Jinx!"

Ever said exactly the same sentence as your friend at the same moment? You share the same language statistics in your heads, so you made the same prediction.

Entropy: measuring surprise

H(X) = − Σx p(x) · log₂ p(x)   =   Σx p(x) · log₂ (1/p(x))Measured in bits with log base 2 (base 10 → hartleys; natural log → nats)

Does it look familiar? It's an expectation, E[X] = Σ x·p(x), where the thing being averaged is surprisal:

surprisal(x) = − log₂ p(x)   ⇒   H = average surprisal = expected surprise
  • Probability 1 → surprisal 0 (no surprise at all).
  • Probability ½ → 1 bit. Probability ¼ → 2 bits. Rarer means more surprising.

Shannon published this in 1948. He built on the earlier Nyquist–Hartley formula (H = n log s), which didn't clearly define what s and n meant.

Common misconception

Plain entropy does not measure the randomness of a whole sequence. It measures the uncertainty of one event (the next symbol). It's also the minimum average number of bits needed to send the outcome efficiently. For sequences we need conditional entropy (next topic).

How redundant is English?

Worked example: bits per letter

To encode the numbers 1–8 in binary you need 3 bits (2³ = 8, so 000–111). For 26 letters you need 5 bits (2⁵ = 32 ≥ 26). With space as a 27th symbol: log₂27 ≈ 4.75 bits.

That's only right if letters were random. Shannon found that in real English text, with about 8 letters of context, the actual information is only about 2.3 bits per character, roughly half. So:

English ≈ 50% redundant
Proof you can read: "Th sntnc s stll rdbl"

Remove all the vowels and you can still read it, like old Nokia-era SMS. That's redundancy at work.

Spotting typos works the same way: you notice a misspelling because it clashes with the statistical patterns stored in your head.

Remove redundancy or add it?

Remove redundancy → efficiency

Compress: why finish "a small oblong reading lamp on the desk" when the end is predictable? Zip files exploit the same idea.

Add redundancy → robustness

If some bits get lost in a noisy channel, the extra information lets you recover the message.

Shannon took these ideas from language, applied them to electrical communication (flip-flops, switches), and published "A Mathematical Theory of Communication" (1948) and later "Prediction and Entropy of Printed English" (1951). Worth reading if you want to see how he thought.

Exam tip

Explain redundancy with an example (q→u, telegrams, vowel-less text), describe what the n-th order approximations show, write the entropy formula and say what its units are, and state the misconception: entropy is about the next event, not the whole sequence.

Try it

Take a 10-word sentence and cover the words one at a time from the end. At which word does guessing become easy? That's the point where entropy drops.

From the lecture slides

  • Shannon's intuition: "…more choices meant more uncertainty, more information." Information is measured as uncertainty: "how much 'choice' is involved in the selection of the event" and "how uncertain we are of the outcome".
  • Entropy builds on the Nyquist and Hartley formula (1928), and Shannon published his entropy in 1948.
  • Activity: quantifying predictability and redundancy. A → SMALL → OBLONG → READING → LAMP → ON → THE → D(esk). Context reduces uncertainty, which is the basic intuition behind language modelling.
  • Efficient communication: "make the chance of error as small as you wish" by either removing redundancy (efficiency) or adding it (error correction, robustness). Shannon worked this out for flip-flop circuits, which led to The Mathematical Theory of Communication (1949): the noisy channel model (see topic 07).
  • Shannon's paper is A Mathematical Theory of Cryptography (1945, declassified 1949). "A stream of data looks like junk! Goal: find the real pattern."

Numericals

From class Worked in the lecture

C1. Bits needed to encode the numbers 1–8

8 distinct values = 2³, so 3 bits (000 … 111). In class some students said 4, but 0–7 and 1–8 are both just 8 values.

C2. Bits needed for the 26 English letters (if random)

2⁴ = 16 < 26 ≤ 32 = 2⁵, so 5 bits. With space as a 27th symbol: log₂27 ≈ 4.75 bits.

C3. Shannon's redundancy estimate

The slide's reasoning: if English were random, every letter would carry the full 5 bits (2⁴ = 16 is not enough, 2⁵ = 32 is). Shannon measured ≈ 2.3 bits per character for text longer than 8 letters, roughly half of 5. So English is about 50% redundant. (Using log₂27 ≈ 4.75 as the maximum gives 1 − 2.3/4.75 ≈ 0.52, the same conclusion.)

C4. Surprisal values

p = 1 → 0 bits · p = ½ → 1 bit · p = ¼ → 2 bits. Rarer means more surprising.

Practice Same level

P1. How many bits do you need to encode 100 different symbols?

2⁶ = 64 < 100 ≤ 128 = 2⁷, so 7 bits.

P2. Surprisal of "u" after "q" if P(u|q) = 0.99, versus a letter with P = 0.001.

−log₂0.99 ≈ 0.0145 bits (almost nothing: redundant). −log₂0.001 ≈ 9.97 bits (very informative).

P3. An alphabet has 50 symbols, and measured information is 2.5 bits per symbol. Redundancy?

Maximum = log₂50 ≈ 5.64 bits. R = 1 − 2.5/5.64 ≈ 0.56 (56%).

P4. Entropy of a next-word distribution {desk: 0.5, table: 0.25, shelf: 0.25}?

H = 0.5·1 + 0.25·2 + 0.25·2 = 1.5 bits.
