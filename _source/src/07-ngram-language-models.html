In 30 seconds

  • A language model (LM) is just a probability distribution over word sequences. It estimates how likely a sentence (or next word) is.
  • We can't know the true probabilities, so we estimate them from a big corpus by counting.
  • Markov assumption: the next word depends only on the last few words. That gives unigram / bigram / trigram models.
  • Bigram estimate: P(w | prev) = count(prev w) / count(prev).
  • Perplexity = 2entropy rate = P(sentence)−1/N: the effective number of choices per word. Lower is better.
  • Unseen sequences get probability 0, so we smooth (add-one/Laplace, <UNK>) and interpolate / back off.

Why should a computer predict words?

Because humans do it constantly. When someone mumbles "I'll be back soon-ish", you hear it correctly instead of "I'll be bassoon-ish", because the first is far more probable. Speech recognition, spelling correction, translation and chatbots all need to know which word sequences are likely.

Example: grammar can't decide, probability can

S1: John went to London in the late afternoon.
S2: John went in the late afternoon to London.
Both are grammatical, yet almost the whole class preferred S1. Grammar can't explain that preference, but probability can. A good LM should give S1 the higher probability. Human preference ∝ probability.

From conditional entropy to language models

In topic 06, H(Y|X) averaged over all values of X. For language we usually care about one specific context, e.g. the uncertainty of the next word after "peanut butter and ___". That's pointwise conditional entropy (one value of X, not the average).

surprisal of a word = − log₂ M(w | history)M = our language model (estimated probability distribution)

Where do the probabilities come from? Not by peering into someone's head. We take a large corpus assumed to represent the language, count how often things occur, and treat those counts as estimates of the true probability.

Key idea

Training a language model = estimating probabilities from a corpus, assuming the corpus is a good enough sample of the language.

From one word to the whole sentence

Apply the chain rule to the sequence w₁ … wN:

total surprisal = − log₂ M(w₁…wN) = Σi − log₂ M(wi | w₁…wi−1)
entropy rate (average surprise per word) = H = (1/N) · [ − log₂ M(w₁…wN) ]

Perplexity

PP = 2H = M(w₁ … wN)−1/N(take log₂ of both sides to see they're the same)
Analogy: a multiple-choice exam

Perplexity 4 means the model is, on average, as confused as if it had to choose among 4 equally likely words at each step. Perplexity 1 means it knows exactly what's next. A perplexed student is a confused student, so lower perplexity = better model (more confident, closer to the true probabilities).

Entropy and perplexity move together. Entropy is "minimum bits / expected surprise". Perplexity expresses the same thing as an "effective number of choices per word".

N-gram models and the Markov assumption

Conditioning on the entire history is impossible (we'd almost never see the same long history twice). Markov assumption: the probability of a word depends only on a fixed number of recent words.

ModelContext usedP(sentence) for "He put the coffee on the table"
Unigram (n=1)noneP(He)·P(put)·P(the)·P(coffee)·…
Bigram (n=2)1 previous wordP(He|<s>)·P(put|He)·P(the|put)·P(coffee|the)·…
Trigram (n=3)2 previous words…·P(the|He put)·P(coffee|put the)·…

<s> and </s> mark the start and end of a sentence. Why learn n-grams when we have transformers? Because RNNs, LSTMs and transformers all build on these ideas, and once n-grams are clear the rest is much easier.

Estimating probabilities by counting (maximum likelihood)

P(wn | wn−1) = C(wn−1 wn) / C(wn−1)     P(wn | wn−2 wn−1) = C(wn−2 wn−1 wn) / C(wn−2 wn−1)

This comes from P(x|y) = P(y,x)/P(y). The total-count denominators cancel and we're left with a ratio of counts. With a big corpus you could literally count with grep -c "put the coffee".

Extra worked example: a full bigram model by hand (the class corpus is in the Numericals section below)

Training corpus (4 sentences):

<s> I like tea </s>
<s> I like coffee </s>
<s> you like tea </s>
<s> I drink tea </s>

Useful counts: C(<s>)=4, C(I)=3, C(like)=3, C(tea)=3 · C(<s> I)=3, C(I like)=2, C(like tea)=2, C(tea </s>)=3.

Test sentence: "I like tea"

FactorCount ratioValue
P(I | <s>)3 / 40.75
P(like | I)2 / 30.667
P(tea | like)2 / 30.667
P(</s> | tea)3 / 31.0
P(sentence)0.75 × 0.667 × 0.667 × 1 = 0.333

Perplexity. What is N? Count the words, and decide about the end marker:

  • PPL (count </s> as a token, N = 3 + 1 = 4): 0.333−1/4 ≈ 1.32
  • PPL1 (words only, N = 3): 0.333−1/3 ≈ 1.44

Count only one boundary tag (</s>), never both. If you know where one sentence ends, you already know where the next begins, so counting <s> too would be redundant. Toolkits like SRILM report both PPL and PPL1.

Unigram version (16 tokens incl. 4 </s>): P = (3/16)(3/16)(3/16)(4/16) ≈ 0.0016, far lower than the bigram's 0.333, because unigrams ignore context.

Why log probabilities?

Multiply thousands of small probabilities and you get numbers like 0.0000000…3 that underflow (floating-point error). Logs turn products into sums: log(a·b) = log a + log b. Toolkits therefore report logprob. (SRILM uses log base 10: log₁₀ 0.333 = −0.477.)

The zero problem and smoothing

Example: "you drink coffee"

P(drink | you) = C(you drink)/C(you) = 0/1 = 0, so the whole sentence gets probability 0 and perplexity becomes infinite. Is the sentence wrong? No. Our corpus is just small. This is sparsity: real sequences missing from the training data.

1. <UNK> for unknown words

Add a special token <UNK> to the vocabulary and map every out-of-vocabulary (OOV) word to it. "Sam I do like linguistics" becomes "Sam I do like <UNK>". The model never sees a truly unknown word again.

2. Add-one (Laplace) smoothing

Pretend every possible event was seen once more than it really was. To keep probabilities summing to 1, add the vocabulary size V to the denominator:

Padd-1(w) = (C(w) + 1) / (N + V)     Padd-1(w | prev) = (C(prev w) + 1) / (C(prev) + V)
Analogy: the fruit bag

Your bag has 2 bananas and 2 apples, and someone asks for an orange. To give the orange a fair (small) chance without changing the overall proportions, you add one of every fruit, not just one orange. That's why we add 1 to every count and V to the total.

Worked example: add-one on our corpus

V = 7 types {I, like, tea, coffee, you, drink, </s>}.

"you drink coffee": (1+1)/(4+7) × (0+1)/(1+7) × (0+1)/(1+7) × (1+1)/(1+7) = 0.182 × 0.125 × 0.125 × 0.25 ≈ 0.00071. Small but not zero.

"I like tea" now: (3+1)/(4+7) × (2+1)/(3+7) × (2+1)/(3+7) × (3+1)/(3+7) ≈ 0.013 (was 0.333).

Smoothing steals probability mass from seen events and gives it to unseen ones. Add-one steals a lot, which is why better methods exist: absolute discounting, Good–Turing, Witten–Bell, Kneser–Ney.

3. Interpolation and back-off

Interpolation: mix all orders with weights that sum to 1:

P̂(w | u, v) = λ₃·P(w | u, v) + λ₂·P(w | v) + λ₁·P(w) ,   λ₁+λ₂+λ₃ = 1

Choose the λs on a held-out (dev) set, as usual in ML: train / dev / test.

Back-off: if the trigram was never seen, fall back to the bigram, then to the unigram. Estimate something, never a blank zero.

Problem with back-off (asked in class)

A zero is sometimes meaningful. "John eats blue" or "I eats" are rare because they're nonsense or ungrammatical, not because the corpus is small. Backing off to the unigram P(blue) gives them a respectable score anyway. Unseen ≠ merely sparse. Sometimes the data is telling you the sequence is improbable.

Evaluating language models

Intrinsic

Likelihood P(test | M) (higher is better), log-likelihood (avoids underflow), perplexity (lower is better).

Extrinsic

Plug the LM into a task: the word-order preference task, speech recognition, machine translation. Does it make the choices humans make?

Golden rules
  • Never train on the test set. The model would have "seen the answers", and its perplexity would be artificially low. That's cheating.
  • Typically unigram PP > bigram PP > trigram PP (e.g. Jurafsky & Martin's Wall Street Journal numbers), because more context means fewer choices.
  • Only compare perplexities of models with the same vocabulary. Comparing two pre-trained LMs with different vocabularies is apples vs oranges.
  • N-grams work well when the test text resembles the training text. In real life it often doesn't, so we need models that generalise and handle unseen words.

Another way to picture an LM: finite-state automata

Words as nodes, transitions as arrows labelled with probabilities. P(he → is) is just the weight on that arrow. This view comes back with HMMs in the next topic. Context-free grammars and dependency trees are yet more ways to represent language structure.

Hands-on: SRILM toolkit

# 1) train a bigram LM (counts + ARPA-format model) with add-one smoothing
ngram-count -text train.txt -order 2 -write train.bigram.count \
            -lm train.bigram.lm -addsmooth 1 -unk

# 2) score test sentences: per-word probabilities, logprob, ppl, ppl1
ngram -lm train.bigram.lm -ppl test.txt -debug 2 -unk

SRILM file formats

  • Count file: each n-gram with its count (e.g. <s> Sam 3, Sam I 3, I am 2).
  • LM (ARPA) file: log₁₀ probability, the n-gram, and the log₁₀ back-off weight.
  • Test output: total logprob (base 10), ppl with N = #words + #</s>, ppl1 with N = #words.

Appendix: Shannon's noisy channel model

message W→encoder→ X →channel p(y|x)→ Y →decoder→Ŵ

There's a duality between compression (remove all redundancy) and transmission accuracy (add controlled redundancy). Channel capacity is reached when the input distribution p(X) maximises I(X;Y).

Î = argmaxi p(i | o) = argmaxi p(i) p(o | i) / p(o) = argmaxi p(i) · p(o | i)p(i) = language model, p(o|i) = channel probability. Used in machine translation, OCR, speech recognition.

In linguistics we can't control the encoding, so we just decode the most likely input. The LM is what makes "I will be back soonish" win over "bassoon dish".

C4. Add-one (Laplace) smoothed bigram, step by step

Rule: P(w | prev) = (C(prev w) + 1) / (C(prev) + |V|), with |V| = 7 (I, am, Sam, like, do, </s>, UNK).

P(Sam|<s>) = (3+1)/(5+7) = 4/12 = 0.333 · P(I|Sam) = 4/12 = 0.333 · P(do|I) = (1+1)/(5+7) = 2/12 = 0.167 · P(like|do) = (1+1)/(2+7) = 2/9 = 0.222 · P(UNK|like) = (0+1)/(3+7) = 1/10 · P(</s>|UNK) = (0+1)/(0+7) = 1/7.

Pure add-one bigram: P(S) = 0.333² × 0.167 × 0.222 × 0.1 × 0.143 ≈ 5.9 × 10⁻⁵ (log₁₀ ≈ −4.23).

The solution sheet instead uses the smoothed unigram values for the two unseen steps (0.034 and 0.207): 0.333² × 0.167 × 0.222 × 0.034 × 0.207 = 2.893 × 10⁻⁵, logprob −4.5387, ppl 5.707, ppl1 8.086. SRILM does something similar: it backs off to the unigram for the unseen bigrams, P(<unk>|like) = 0.0294 and P(</s>|<unk>) = 0.207, giving −4.60133. Either way, the smoothed bigram beats the smoothed unigram (ppl ≈ 5.8 vs 7.8).
