In 30 seconds

  • One-hot vectors are huge, sparse, and every pair is orthogonal, so "hotel" and "motel" look no more similar than "hotel" and "banana".
  • Distributional hypothesis (J.R. Firth): "You shall know a word by the company it keeps." Meaning comes from context.
  • Word embeddings are short, dense vectors (e.g. 100–300 numbers). Similar words get nearby vectors, and arithmetic works: king − man + woman ≈ queen.
  • word2vec (skip-gram) slides a window over text and learns vectors so that the centre word predicts its context words. CBOW does the reverse.
  • P(context o | centre c) = softmax of dot products: exp(uo·vc) / Σw exp(uw·vc). Train with cross-entropy loss.

What's wrong with one-hot vectors?

With a vocabulary of V words, each word is a V-long vector with a single 1.

Example: hotel vs motel

hotel = [0 0 0 1 0 0 … 0], motel = [0 0 0 0 0 1 … 0]. Their dot product (and cosine similarity) is 0, so they look completely unrelated, even though they mean almost the same thing.

  • No notion of similarity: all words are equally far apart, with no semantic structure.
  • Huge and sparse: modern models have vocabularies of roughly 250k tokens, so each one-hot vector is 250k long and almost all zeros. That's slow and memory-hungry.

The big idea: represent a word by its context

Distributional hypothesis

"You shall know a word by the company it keeps." (J.R. Firth). A word's meaning is spread across the words that often appear near it. One of the most successful ideas in modern statistical NLP.

Example: bank

"I went to the bank to draw some money" → money, draw, account nearby means a financial bank.
"I want to sit near the river bank" → river, sit, water nearby means a riverbank.

The context of a word = the words within a window around it (e.g. ±2 words).

Dense vectors (word embeddings)

Instead of [0, 0, 1, 0, …] with 250,000 entries, we want something like

banking = [ 0.286, 0.792, −0.177, −0.107, 0.109, −0.542, 0.349, 0.271 ]

These are called word vectors, word embeddings or neural word representations: a kind of distributed representation. Like PCA or other dimensionality reduction, they keep the meaning in far fewer dimensions. The numbers can be negative or positive because they are simply learned weights.

What can we do with them?

  • Visualise: reduce to 2-D and similar words cluster (come, go, give, keep / has, have, had).
  • Measure similarity with dot product or cosine similarity.
  • Do arithmetic:
vec(king) − vec(man) + vec(woman) ≈ vec(queen)
Analogy: a map of meaning

Picture words as cities on a map. "king → queen" is the same journey (direction and distance) as "man → woman". That arrow is roughly a "gender direction". Current AI-safety research uses similar arithmetic inside LLMs, for example finding a "harmful vs harmless request" direction ("how to make a bomb" vs "how to make a flower pot") to understand when a model refuses.

word2vec (Mikolov et al., 2013)

A framework for learning word vectors from a large corpus.

  1. Every word in a fixed vocabulary starts with a random vector.
  2. Go through each position t in the text: there's a centre word c and surrounding context ("outside") words o.
  3. Use the similarity of the vectors for c and o to compute P(o | c).
  4. Adjust the vectors to make that probability higher for words that really occur together.

Skip-gram

Given the centre word, predict the context words.
"into" → {problems, turning, banking, crises}

CBOW (continuous bag of words)

Given the context words, predict the centre word.
{problems, turning, banking, crises} → "into"

Example: the sliding window (size 2)

"… problems turning into banking crises …"

Centre = into: predict problems, turning (left) and banking, crises (right), i.e. P(wt−2|wt), P(wt−1|wt), P(wt+1|wt), P(wt+2|wt). Then slide one step: centre = banking, and so on.

The objective

Likelihood: how probable is the whole corpus's context words given the centre words, under parameters θ (all the vectors)?

L(θ) = Πt=1..T Π−m ≤ j ≤ m, j≠0 P(wt+j | wt ; θ)

Multiplying so many probabilities is impractical, so take the log (products become sums) and average. Then minimise the negative:

J(θ) = − (1/T) Σt Σj log P(wt+j | wt ; θ)

Minimising J = maximising predictive accuracy. Like maximum likelihood estimation, but over words. It's next-token prediction in disguise.

Where P(o | c) comes from

Each word gets two vectors (two roles, not two meanings): vw when it's the centre word and uw when it's a context word.

P(o | c) = exp(uo · vc) / Σw∈V exp(uw · vc)
  • Numerator: dot product = similarity of centre and context word. Bigger dot product, bigger probability.
  • Denominator: normalise over the whole vocabulary so the probabilities sum to 1.
  • Why exp? Dot products can be negative, and exp makes everything positive. It also makes big similarities much bigger and small ones shrink quickly.
  • This whole expression is the softmax function.
Sigmoid or softmax?

Sigmoid for a binary decision (yes/no). Softmax for choosing one out of many, e.g. which of 10 (or 250k) vocabulary words comes next.

The skip-gram network as matrices

Worked example (exam style, from the practice session)

Vocabulary of 4 words, d = 2. The centre word is word #3, so x = [0, 0, 1, 0].

  1. Lookup: vc = Eᵀx = the 3rd row of E = [0.4, 0.1] (say).
  2. Scores: multiply by the context matrix U (4 × 2) to get one score per vocabulary word, e.g. [0.02, 0.03, −0.14, 0.09].
  3. Softmax → roughly [0.25, 0.25, 0.22, 0.27]. The highest is word #4, the predicted context word.
  4. Final embedding of the centre word after this single forward pass (no update yet): still [0.4, 0.1]. In word2vec the learned rows of E are the embeddings.

These vectors are static: "bank" has the same vector in "river bank" and "money bank". Contextual embeddings (BERT, transformers) fix this.

Cross-entropy loss

Compare the predicted distribution p with the gold one-hot y:

CE = − Σi yi · log pi   =   − log p(true word)
Worked example

Gold y = [0, 1, 0]. Prediction p = [0.7, 0.2, 0.1].

CE = −(0·log 0.7 + 1·log 0.2 + 0·log 0.1) = −log 0.2 ≈ 1.61 (natural log) or 2.32 bits

If the model had given the true word 0.9, CE = −ln 0.9 ≈ 0.105. Confident and correct means a small loss. The loss is then back-propagated to update E and U.

Implementation outline (what you coded in class) 
corpus = ["cats like milk", "cats drink milk", "dogs like milk"]
vocab  = sorted({w for s in corpus for w in s.split()})
w2i    = {w:i for i,w in enumerate(vocab)}

# 1) training pairs (centre, context) with window = 1
pairs = []
for s in corpus:
    ids = [w2i[w] for w in s.split()]
    for i, c in enumerate(ids):
        for j in range(max(0, i-1), min(len(ids), i+2)):
            if j != i: pairs.append((c, ids[j]))

# 2) parameters: E (V x d) centre vectors, U (V x d) context vectors
import numpy as np
V, d, lr = len(vocab), 3, 0.05
E, U = np.random.randn(V, d)*0.1, np.random.randn(V, d)*0.1

for epoch in range(200):
    for c, o in pairs:
        v = E[c]                         # lookup
        scores = U @ v                   # V scores
        p = np.exp(scores - scores.max()); p /= p.sum()   # softmax
        loss = -np.log(p[o])             # cross-entropy
        g = p.copy(); g[o] -= 1          # dLoss/dscores
        E[c] -= lr * (U.T @ g)           # back-prop to centre vector
        U    -= lr * np.outer(g, v)      # and to context vectors

From the lecture slides

  • Why not WordNet synonyms? The slide says relying on them "fails badly": synonym lists are incomplete, similarity isn't binary, and context changes meaning. So instead, learn to encode similarity in the vectors themselves. Example: a search for "Seattle motel" should match "Seattle hotel".
  • One-hot dimension = vocabulary size (e.g. 500,000+), a localist representation.
  • Contexts that build the meaning of banking: "…government debt problems turning into banking crises…", "…Europe needs unified banking regulation…", "…India has just given its banking system a shot in the arm…".
  • Why two vectors per word? Easier optimisation. Average them at the end.
  • Loss variants: naive softmax (simple but expensive with many classes), hierarchical softmax, negative sampling.
  • Stochastic gradient descent: J(θ) covers all windows in the corpus (billions), so instead of waiting, sample a window or mini-batch and update after each one.
  • Softmax name: "max" because it amplifies the largest xᵢ, "soft" because smaller ones still get some probability.

Slide pseudocode: WORD2VEC-SKIPGRAM(Corpus, c, d, η, T) 
1. Build vocabulary V from the corpus
2. Initialise input embeddings E randomly
3. Initialise output weights W randomly
4. For each sentence, each word w at position i,
   each context position j within window c (i ≠ j): add pair (w, context_word)
5. For epoch = 1..T: shuffle pairs; for each (center, context):
     h = E[center]                     # forward
     scores = h × W
     probabilities = Softmax(scores)
     loss = -log(probabilities[context])
     error = probabilities; error[context] -= 1   # backprop
     gradient_W = hᵀ × error ; gradient_h = W × error
     W = W - η × gradient_W ; E[center] = E[center] - η × gradient_h
6. Return E

  • Parameters: input matrix E is V × d = 7 × 3 = 21, output matrix W is d × V = 3 × 7 = 21 → 42 numbers.
  • Loss at the start: with tiny random weights the softmax is almost uniform, P(context) ≈ 1/7, so the loss is −ln(1/7) = ln 7 ≈ 1.95. That is exactly where the loss curve above starts.
  • Best possible loss: "like" has two different correct contexts in the same sentence (cats, milk), so P can't be 1 for both. The loss can never reach 0.
